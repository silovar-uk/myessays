# Is it normal to talk to yourself?

[0:07] As your morning alarm blares, you mutter to yourself,
[0:10] “Why did I set it so early?”
[0:13] While brushing your teeth, you think,
[0:15] “I need a haircut... unless?”
[0:18] Rushing out the front door, you reach for your keys
[0:21] and realize they’re not there.
[0:23] Frustrated you shout, “I can’t do anything right!”
[0:28] just in time to notice your neighbor. What is SelfTalk
[0:30] Being caught talking to yourself can feel embarrassing,
[0:34] and some people even stigmatize this behavior as a sign of mental instability.
[0:39] But decades of psychology research show that talking to yourself
[0:43] is completely normal.
[0:45] In fact, most, if not all, of us engage in some form of self-talk
[0:50] every single day.
[0:52] So why do we talk to ourselves?
[0:55] And does what we say matter?
[0:58] Self-talk refers to the narration inside your head,
[1:01] sometimes called inner speech.
[1:04] It differs from mental imagery or recalling facts and figures.
[1:08] Specifically, psychologists define self-talk
[1:11] as verbalized thoughts directed toward yourself or some facet of your life.
[1:17] This includes personal conversations like “I need to work on my free throw.”
[1:21] But it also includes reflections you have throughout the day,
[1:24] like “The gym is crowded tonight. I’ll come back tomorrow.” Early SelfTalk
[1:28] And while most self-talk in adults tends to be silent,
[1:31] speaking to yourself out loud also falls into this category.
[1:35] In fact, psychologists believe our first experiences with self-talk
[1:40] are mostly vocal,
[1:41] as children often speak to themselves out loud as they play.
[1:45] In the 1930s, Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky hypothesized
[1:49] that this kind of speech was actually key to development.
[1:53] By repeating conversations they’ve had with adults,
[1:55] children practice managing their behaviors and emotions on their own. Internal SelfTalk
[2:01] Then, as they grow older, this outward self-talk tends to become internalized,
[2:06] morphing into a private inner dialogue.
[2:09] We know this internal self-talk is important,
[2:12] and can help you plan, work through difficult situations,
[2:14] and even motivate you throughout the day.
[2:17] But studying self-talk can be difficult.
[2:20] It relies on study subjects clearly tracking a behavior that’s spontaneous
[2:25] and often done without conscious control.
[2:27] For this reason, scientists are still working to answer basic questions,
[2:31] like, why do some people self-talk more than others?
[2:34] What areas of the brain are activated during self-talk?
[2:38] And how does this activation differ from normal conversation? Benefits of SelfTalk
[2:42] One thing we know for certain, however,
[2:45] is that what you say in these conversations can have real impacts
[2:50] on your attitude and performance.
[2:53] Engaging in self-talk that’s instructional or motivational
[2:56] has been shown to increase focus, boost self-esteem,
[3:00] and help tackle everyday tasks.
[3:03] For example, one study of collegiate tennis players
[3:07] found that incorporating instructional self-talk into practice
[3:11] increased their concentration and accuracy.
[3:15] And just as chatting to a friend can help decrease stress,
[3:18] speaking directly to yourself may also help you regulate your emotions.
[3:24] Distanced self-talk is when you talk to yourself,
[3:28] as if in conversation with another person. Distanced SelfTalk
[3:30] So, rather than “I’m going to crush this exam,”
[3:34] you might think, “Caleb, you are prepared for this test!”
[3:38] One study found that this kind of self-talk was especially beneficial
[3:42] for reducing stress when engaging in anxiety-inducing tasks,
[3:47] such as meeting new people or public speaking.
[3:50] But where positive self-talk can help you, negative self-talk can harm you.
[3:55] Most people are critical of themselves occasionally,
[3:59] but when this behavior gets too frequent or excessively negative,
[4:02] it can become toxic.
[4:04] High levels of negative self-talk are often predictive
[4:08] of anxiety in children and adults.
[4:10] And those who constantly blame themselves for their problems
[4:13] and ruminate on those situations
[4:15] typically experience more intense feelings of depression.
[4:20] Today, there’s a field of psychological treatment
[4:22] called cognitive behavioral therapy, or CBT,
[4:27] which is partially focused on regulating the tone of self-talk.
[4:31] Cognitive behavioral therapists often teach strategies
[4:35] to identify cycles of negative thoughts
[4:37] and replace them with neutral or more compassionate reflections.
[4:42] Over time, these tools can improve one's mental health.
[4:47] So the next time you find yourself chatting with yourself,
[4:50] remember to be kind.
[4:53] That inner voice is a partner you’ll be talking to for many years to come.
