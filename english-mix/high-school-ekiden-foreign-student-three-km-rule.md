# Why only 3km?――「外国人留学生の特性」から高校駅伝のルールを読む

## 1. Three kilometers. The number is clear, but the reason is “characteristics”

全国高校駅伝には、strangely specificなルールがある。Foreign students can be entered up to two, but only one may actually race. Since the 2024 event, boys can run only leg 2 or 5, and girls only leg 3 or 4. Every eligible leg is **3km**.

The number is extremely clear: 3km. Not 2.9, not 4. Yet the December 2023 explanation from the event committee suddenly becomes foggier. After mentioning possible benefits such as globalization of high-school education and stronger Japanese athletes, it uses the phrase **「外国人留学生の特性」— “the characteristics of foreign students.”**

Characteristics.

That is suddenly a very large word. Does it mean height, running ability, training background, or their institutional position as international students? The ruler is drawn precisely at 3km, while the reason for placing that ruler has softer edges.

That mismatch became my starting point. And once I researched it, the simple explanation — “Kenyan students are too fast, so they were moved to a shorter leg” — turned out to be half right and half insufficient.

## 2. First correction: the 3km rule is not a Hakone Ekiden rule

The video that triggered this essay moves among Hakone Ekiden, high-school ekiden and corporate ekiden while introducing Hidekazu Izumi’s book *アフリカから来たランナーたち――箱根駅伝のケニア人留学生*. In that flow, it is easy to remember the 3km restriction as if it were “Hakone’s foreign-student rule.” I did that at first too.

But these rules must be separated. **The 3km restriction belongs to the National High School Ekiden, not the Hakone Ekiden.** The 2025 high-school event guidelines list the boys’ 42.195km and girls’ 21.0975km courses and explicitly restrict foreign students to designated 3km legs.

By contrast, Hakone Ekiden’s official site casually notes that the famous second leg is often used for foreign students. That leg is very much not 3km. So the broad topic — Kenyan student runners in Japanese ekiden — is connected to the high-school restriction, but they are not one institution.

This sounds like a small correction, but it changes the argument. When criticizing a system, merging rules from different competitions into one vague “will of the ekiden world” does not remove fog. It creates new fog of our own.

## 3. Put the rules on a timeline, and you see “managing impact” more than simple exclusion

According to the official National High School Ekiden material, explicit limits on foreign students were introduced from the 1995 event: up to two could be entered, but only one could race. From 2008, that one runner could no longer run the first leg. From 2024, foreign students were limited to the shortest 3km legs for both boys and girls.

Read only as a timeline, the gate appears to keep narrowing. But the same document says surveys were sent to prefectural high-school athletics bodies in 2019 and 2021, and in both cases a majority supported specifying the legs in which foreign students could participate. The 3km rule was not simply invented overnight by one person; it came through a governance process within the sport.

Another point matters even more: participation was not reduced to zero. The system limited numbers, removed leg 1, then moved the eligible runner to the shortest leg. At least in institutional form, this is not a design that says “do not participate.” It looks more like a design that says **participate, but with a smaller effect on the overall race**.

The question therefore shifts. It is not only “accept or exclude.” It is: once athletes are accepted, how much of their competitive power should the event allow to become visible? Three kilometers is where that negotiation currently lands.

## 4. Later explanation makes the “characteristic” surprisingly explicit: speed

If you read only the official PDF, 「外国人留学生の特性」looks vague. But a contemporary report by *Monthly Athletics / 月陸Online* includes an explanation from the head of the national high-school athletics technical committee. One of the characteristics named there is clearly **speed**.

The basic idea was this: because speed development matters in the junior years, foreign students should show that speed in the shortest leg, and Japanese high-school runners should challenge them there, potentially raising the Japanese side’s speed as well. So the word “characteristics” was not completely empty.

Okay, case closed — except it is not.

If speed is the central issue, the rule does not actually measure “speed.” It measures whether someone is a foreign student. A spectacularly fast Japanese runner may run the 10km first leg. A foreign student who is not exceptionally fast is still limited to 3km. The system is not restricting individual performance; it is **using status as a proxy for an expected performance gap**.

Governance often uses proxies because reality is complicated. But proxies draw lines that may not fit each individual. Once “characteristic = speed” becomes visible, the question does not disappear. It becomes sharper: why is speed being managed through nationality / student-status categories?

## 5. So I actually calculated what 3km means

“Only the shortest 3km leg” sounds as if the rule limits foreign-student impact to roughly the same share in boys’ and girls’ races. So I calculated it. In the boys’ 42.195km race, 3km is about **7.1%**. In the girls’ 21.0975km race, the same 3km is about **14.2%**.

That is almost exactly double.

Of course, distance share is not the same thing as competitive impact. Hills, race order, athlete type and when a time gap is created all matter. Still, the same 3km produces very different shares of the total race.

So this does not look like a mathematical rule saying “foreign students may influence up to X percent of the race.” It is much more practical: **choose the shortest existing leg in each course**.

That changes the meaning of 3km again. It looks less like a precise answer to fairness and more like an administratively clear landing point for several competing goals.

## 6. Let’s stress-test the rule with four slightly annoying questions

**Question 1: If “competitive balance” is the goal, why not regulate speed itself?** A rule based on records or rankings could treat exceptional athletes the same regardless of nationality. But then a strange incentive appears: become faster and you lose access to longer legs. The threshold would also need constant maintenance. “Foreign student” is a crude proxy, but it is much easier to administer.

**Question 2: If “education” is the goal, why adjust educational opportunity by distance?** High-school sport can be part of education, but educational value and leg length are not identical. On the other hand, allowing a 3km national-championship appearance preserves more opportunity than a total ban. Here, education seems less like the reason for restriction and more like a brake against restricting too far.

**Question 3: If school resource inequality is the problem, why target only foreign students?** Recruiting strong athletes from overseas can involve school networks and financial resources. Yet domestic powerhouse schools also differ greatly in coaching, facilities, dormitories and recruiting power. The 3km rule does not solve all unequal resources; it isolates one especially visible form of them.

**Question 4: Is “making the race entertaining” also a goal?** There is not enough evidence to claim that. The official explanation does not cite ratings or drama. But in ekiden, a time gap created by one runner is literally handed to the next runner through the sash. Because one exceptional performance can visibly reshape the whole race, it is understandable that leg placement becomes a recurring governance issue.

Put all four together and no single answer explains everything. Competitive balance, education, resource inequality and the way a race looks to spectators all overlap. Three kilometers is not the pure implementation of one ideal; it is what remains after multiple values collide.

## 7. In ekiden, one person’s speed easily becomes the story of the whole team

Why does this issue appear so sharply in ekiden? Football and baseball also have players born abroad. But ekiden has a brutally legible display system that many team sports do not: **time gaps**.

One runner gains 30 seconds. The next runner starts 30 seconds ahead. The advantage is not dissolved; it is stored in the sash. Meanwhile, each leg’s rank and time are displayed next to an individual name. A team sport becomes unusually easy to read as a sequence of individual contributions.

In that structure, an outstanding foreign student can make viewers notice “that one runner is fast” before they notice “this team is strong.” By contrast, long-term effects — teammates getting faster through daily training together, adapting to a stronger standard, building a team at school — do not appear in the split table.

So the foreign-student debate is not only about ability gaps. It is also about **what becomes visible in television and result sheets, and what stays invisible**. That is exactly where Hidekazu Izumi’s book becomes useful.

## 8. Don’t turn Kenyan runners into a device labeled “fast person”

The starting point of Hidekazu Izumi’s *アフリカから来たランナーたち* is a discomfort with that visibility. Bunshun’s introduction asks why we know so little about the families, hometowns, routes to Japan and lives after graduation of Kenyan students whose running we repeatedly watch on television. Izumi traveled to Kenya three times to report on the runners and people around them.

That matters for rule design too. Treat 「外国人留学生」as one card, and the policy problem becomes easy: fast, high impact, therefore 3km. Done.

But actual athletes can be trying to reach elite competition, support family, study in Japan, or pursue several of those goals at once. Schools also mix motives: winning, development, international exchange and institutional visibility. Human motives are plural, while rules create a singular character called “the foreign student.”

This is where the jokes should stop. We can debate competition rules. But when that debate becomes contempt toward nationality or origin, the topic has moved from competitive fairness to attacking a person. **The roughness of a rule must not be converted into an individual athlete’s blame.**

## 9. Re-reading Yamanashi Gakuin’s 1992 win from the result sheet

In the Hakone context, Yamanashi Gakuin University’s first title in 1992 is symbolic. The official Hakone record shows Joseph Otsuori finishing second on leg 2 and Kennedy Isena winning leg 3. It is impossible to look at those numbers and say the foreign students were irrelevant.

But keep reading the same table. Yamanashi Gakuin also won legs 6 and 8, finished second on leg 7 and third on leg 10. Compress the victory into one sentence — “two foreign students won it for them” — and the rest of the same record sheet disappears.

The opposite simplification is also weak: “everyone contributed, therefore the foreign-student effect does not matter.” Both can be true. Otsuori and Isena were major competitive forces, and the team as a whole was strong.

That coexistence is what makes ekiden hard to talk about. We should not ignore an exceptional individual contribution, but we also should not use one individual to explain the entire team. **Ekiden adds individual performances, while spectators quickly multiply them into stories.**

## 10. Hiding “we want to win” may make the whole thing harder to understand

The most interesting phrase in the video was the idea of failing to control or state “desire.” Schools want to win. Athletes want to become faster. Some runners want sport to open a path in life. The event wants to remain educational. At the same time, organizers want a competition that feels reasonably balanced.

None of those desires is automatically suspicious. The trouble starts when we try to package all of them inside one clean word: not only “international exchange,” not only “education,” not only “fairness,” and certainly not only “foreign students are too fast.”

What I found in the 3km rule was less a single person’s bad intent than **the awkwardness of a system trying to satisfy several legitimate desires at once**. That is why the official word 「特性」still bothers me. The problem is not merely that it is vague. It looks like a trace left when several values that would conflict if stated separately are bundled into one term.

In that sense, “darkness” is probably too dramatic. The reality is more ordinary and more difficult: welcome athletes, protect competition, remain educational, keep the race meaningful. When all of those aims are translated into a rule, you sometimes get a strangely precise number like 3km.

## 11. Before researching, 3km looked like a simple “make the fast runner run less” number

Before researching, I treated 3km as performance adjustment. Kenyan students are fast. Long legs create large gaps. Therefore move them to a short leg. Clean and simple.

That element did not disappear after research. The explanation really does mention speed, and the restrictions did tighten step by step in 1995, 2008 and 2024.

But 3km is doing more than that. The same distance means 7.1% of the boys’ race and 14.2% of the girls’ race. The line is drawn by “foreign-student” status rather than individual speed. Participation remains possible. Challenging foreign students is itself offered as a benefit for Japanese athletes. Education and competition are packed into the same rule.

So now, 3km no longer looks like the answer.

**Three kilometers looks like the runnable length of a question Japanese high-school ekiden has not fully answered yet.**

The next time a foreign student runner appears in an ekiden broadcast, I will probably look beyond how fast that athlete is and ask why they are running there. And at the same time, I will ask why they can run only there. Seeing both questions together may be the moment the runner stops being a symbol on the screen and becomes visible as a person.
