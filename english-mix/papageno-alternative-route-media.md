# パパゲーノは「死ななかった脇役」から、a name for another route になった
## From Mozart’s *The Magic Flute* to the Papageno effect――物語はfactsだけでなく、possible next actionsも伝える

“Papageno” is not a name most people can explain on sight.

モーツァルトの『魔笛』に出てくる鳥刺しの男。He likes food, love, ordinary comfort, and would rather avoid heroic trials. 王子でも賢者でもない。少し臆病で、見栄も張り、weaknessを隠し切れない。

Yet more than two hundred years later, his name entered suicide-prevention and media research. **The Papageno effect** describes the possible protective impact of stories that show people coping with suicidal crises through support, alternatives, and recovery.

At first, the jump feels strange: from an eighteenth-century opera to public-health research.

But the bridge is surprisingly clear.

**Papageno matters not because he never despairs, but because despair does not remain the only available route.**

> **Information date: 2 October 2026**
>
> This essay explains cultural history and media research. It avoids detailed descriptions of suicide methods. Research findings are separated from later practical interpretation.

## 1. Papageno was designed as an ordinary human beside symbolic heroes

そもそも『魔笛』とは何か。

*Die Zauberflöte* (*The Magic Flute*) is a two-act work composed by ヴォルフガング・アマデウス・モーツァルト（Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart） with a libretto by エマヌエル・シカネーダー（Emanuel Schikaneder）. It premiered in Vienna on September 30, 1791.

Its form is called **ジングシュピール（Singspiel）**, a German-language “song-play” that moves between spoken dialogue and musical numbers. In other words, it was not only elevated court art; it was also popular theater.

The opera contains highly symbolic figures such as Tamino, the Queen of the Night, and Sarastro. Papageno lives closer to the ground. 彼は鳥を捕って暮らし、腹が減るのを嫌い、恋人を欲しがり、dangerous initiation ritualsにはなるべく入りたくない。

The Metropolitan Opera connects him to **ハンスヴルスト（Hanswurst）**, a comic folk-theater type: crafty, simple, physical, and close to the audience. Schikaneder himself played Papageno in the original production, while Mozart conducted.

This matters because Papageno is not a model of perfect self-control.

He is an **identification surface** for ordinary weakness.

後に自殺予防研究が彼の名を借りたのは、弱さのない人物だからではない。**弱さを抱えたまま、another action becomes visible to him.**

参照：[Metropolitan Opera, Plot and Creation](https://www.metopera.org/discover/education/educator-guides/the-magic-flute/the-operas-plot-and-creation/)

![Playbill for the first performance of The Magic Flute, 1791](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/63/Zauberfl%C3%B6te-Theaterzettel1791.jpg)

## 2. The key scene is not “be strong”; it is “there is still another move”

Near the end of the opera, Papageno believes he has lost Papagena and falls into despair.

His perceived future narrows. Then the Three Boys intervene and remind him that another action is available. 彼は忘れていた道具を使い、the situation changes, and he is reunited with Papagena.

The interesting part is what does **not** happen.

Papageno does not solve the crisis through heroic willpower. He does not suddenly become wiser than everyone else. **Another person interrupts the closed loop and restores options.**

Calling this merely “a hopeful ending” misses something. Hope is an emotional state. What changes in the scene is closer to an **action set**: a future that looked like one route becomes more than one route.

That is why Papageno later became useful as a research metaphor.

The underlying idea is not “positive thinking saves people.” It is closer to this:

**In a crisis, showing a workable alternative can change what the person can imagine doing next.**

参照：[WHO, Preventing suicide: a resource for media professionals, update 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240076846)

## 3. Before the Papageno effect, research focused on harmful imitation: the Werther effect

The Papageno effect was built as a conceptual counterpart to the **ウェルテル効果（Werther effect）**.

Werther is the protagonist of Goethe’s 1774 novel *The Sorrows of Young Werther*. Stories have long circulated that the novel was followed by imitative suicides across Europe. The historical scale is disputed, so it is safer to treat the famous “wave” as partly contested history rather than a clean statistic.

The modern research term comes from sociologist デイヴィッド・フィリップス（David P. Phillips）. In 1974, he analysed suicide stories in British and American newspapers and reported increases in suicide after heavily publicized cases.

Later research strengthened the broader concern: celebrity suicide coverage, repeated exposure, sensational presentation, and detailed descriptions of methods can be associated with subsequent imitative behaviour.

But this is not a “media controls people” theory.

Suicide is multi-causal. 社会、経済、文化、心理、生活上の出来事が交差する。Media exposure is one factor among many, and effects differ across people and contexts.

Still, the Werther-effect literature created an important reversal question:

**If stories can model harmful behaviour, can stories also model adaptive coping?**

That question opens the door to Papageno.

参照：[Phillips, 1974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4844994/)

## 4. In 2010, researchers gave “crisis mastery” a name: the Papageno effect

In 2010, Thomas Niederkrotenthaler and colleagues analysed **497 suicide-related print-media reports** published in Austria from January through June 2005 and compared their content with short-term changes in suicide rates.

Some content patterns were associated with increases. Repetitive reporting of the same suicide and reporting that repeated suicide myths were positively associated with suicide rates.

Other content moved in the opposite direction. Stories about individuals who experienced suicidal thoughts without suicidal behaviour were associated with lower suicide rates.

A latent-class analysis also identified a **“mastery of crisis”** class: stories about people who used coping strategies other than suicidal behaviour under adverse circumstances. This class was negatively associated with suicide.

The researchers called this possible protective direction the **Papageno effect**.

Four terms help keep the concept clean:

- **ウェルテル効果（Werther effect）**: media-related imitation associated with increased suicidal behaviour.
- **パパゲーノ効果（Papageno effect）**: possible protective effects of media narratives of coping, hope, and recovery.
- **危機の克服（mastery of crisis）**: getting through a crisis using alternatives to suicidal behaviour.
- **当事者経験（lived experience）**: first-hand experience of the issue being discussed.

But the original study was ecological and observational. It did not randomize individuals to read different stories. 誰がどの記事を読んだかまでは追えない。

So 2010 did not close the case.

**It converted an intuition into a testable research question.**

参照：[Niederkrotenthaler et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20807970/)

## 5. Later experiments suggest a small benefit, not a magic protective shield

A useful concept survives by becoming more precise.

In 2022, *The Lancet Public Health* published an individual-participant-data meta-analysis of randomized trials testing media stories of hope and recovery.

For the primary analysis on suicidal ideation among people with higher vulnerability, six studies contributed follow-up data from 569 participants. The pooled standardized mean difference was **-0.22**, indicating a small reduction in suicidal ideation in the intervention group.

A standardized mean difference lets studies using different scales be compared. -0.22 is not a dramatic transformation. It is better read as **a small but detectable effect**.

For help-seeking attitudes and intentions, four studies showed no clear difference between intervention and control groups.

This distinction matters.

A story may change suicidal thoughts a little without immediately changing help-seeking behaviour. It certainly does not follow that a small experimental effect automatically produces a measurable drop in population mortality.

So the evidence supports a narrower claim:

**Stories showing concrete coping and recovery may reduce suicidal ideation in some vulnerable audiences, but the effect is modest and not universal.**

参照：[Niederkrotenthaler et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35122759/)

## 6. “Positive story = safe story” is one of the biggest misunderstandings

The word *effect* sounds cleaner than the evidence.

Different studies use different media: newspapers, educational websites, videos, social posts, fictional scenes. They also use different definitions: hope, recovery, mastery, lived experience, coping.

The audience varies too.

A randomized trial among psychiatric patients with affective disorders suggested benefits from crisis-mastery stories, but it also observed a temporary deterioration in mood immediately after exposure. The authors explicitly called for more research on which groups might experience unintended effects.

And outcomes are not interchangeable. Suicidal ideation, help-seeking intention, actual help-seeking, suicide attempts, and population mortality are different endpoints.

ここからは筆者の解釈になるが、one more risk deserves attention: **forced positivity**.

If recovery stories are edited into “ask for help and everything gets better,” they can erase relapse, ambivalence, structural problems, and the slow work of recovery. A clean inspirational arc can become another form of pressure.

The useful version of Papageno is therefore not “make it uplifting.”

It is:

**Do not erase complexity, but keep alternatives visible.**

参照：[Till et al., randomized controlled trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34732271/)

## 7. WHO guidance changes the structure of reporting, not merely its tone

The World Health Organization updated its media resource in 2023. 日本語版も公開されている。

WHO recommends proactive reporting about coping, survival, hope, recovery, and help-seeking. At the same time, it warns against sensationalism, detailed descriptions of suicide methods, oversimplifying causes, and careless treatment of celebrity suicides. It also recommends including information about where people can seek help.

One recommendation says:

> “Do report stories of how to cope with life stressors and/or suicidal thoughts and the importance of help-seeking.”

The deeper point is structural.

Responsible reporting is not only about deleting dangerous details. It is also about **what remains after editing**.

What did the person do? Who helped? What support existed? What changed over time? Where can the reader go next?

In that sense, reporting ethics is not simply a list of prohibitions.

**It is information architecture for a person whose perceived options may already be narrow.**

参照：[WHO 2023](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240076846) ／ [WHO Japanese edition](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/372691/9789240076846-jpn.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=10)

## 8. Social media turns Papageno from newsroom ethics into everyday publishing ethics

The original 2010 study was about newspapers. Today, crisis narratives circulate through feeds, short video, influencers, fan communities, corporate accounts, and streaming fiction.

A 2025 experiment with 354 participants tested social-media posts about hope, healing, and recovery by an influencer with lived experience of a suicide attempt. Exposure was associated with reduced suicidal thoughts, with stronger reductions among participants who began with higher levels of suicidal thoughts. Help-seeking intention also increased.

A 2026 systematic review of social-media research concluded that both Werther- and Papageno-type effects can occur online, while noting substantial variation in methods, platforms, users, and content.

This changes who counts as “the media.”

新聞社だけではない。A creator, community manager, sports club, brand account, school, or individual can all become publishers of crisis narratives.

The ethical question travels with the publishing power.

**Once everyone can publish, responsible narrative design is no longer a newsroom-only skill.**

参照：[Social media influencers and the Papageno effect, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40010233/) ／ [Social-media systematic review, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38848950/)

## 9. For PR and editorial work, ask whether the story leaves usable alternatives

ここからはpractical proposal。

When a company, sports organization, school, municipality, or media outlet tells a story about mental-health crisis or recovery, the temptation is to produce a clean emotional arc.

Papageno suggests a different editorial test.

Do not ask first, “Is this inspiring?”

Ask:

- Have we reduced a complex crisis to one cause?
- Are concrete coping strategies and support routes more visible than dramatic crisis details?
- Does the story imply that recovery happened because the person was unusually strong?
- Can the audience identify an accessible next step or support resource?
- Are we turning someone’s lived experience into brand content?
- Do consent, dignity, and future redistribution remain protected?

The last point matters because a potentially helpful story is still someone’s life.

A public-health rationale does not erase editorial power.

**Responsible storytelling asks not only what the story does to an audience, but also what publishing does to the person whose story it is.**

## 10. Papageno’s lesson is not “have hope”; it is “do not collapse the map to one route”

At first, Papageno feels like a strange name for a research concept.

Why should a comic bird-catcher from a 1791 opera matter to modern media ethics?

After following the history, the choice begins to make sense.

Papageno is not a sage. He is not psychologically invulnerable. He becomes desperate, loses perspective, and needs interruption from outside. Then another action becomes visible.

The 2010 research moved that structure from the stage to media.

Stories do not only say what happened. They also leave behind models of what people do in situations like this.

A story full of death details can narrow the imagined script. A story that shows coping, support, time, and alternatives can widen it. The evidence does not justify claiming that one story can save everyone. The 2022 meta-analysis found a small effect, and not every outcome improved.

Yet the larger insight remains useful:

**Media distributes not only information, but action possibilities.**

That is the real intellectual value of the Papageno effect.

It is less a command to “be hopeful” than a discipline of editing: when a person’s world has become narrow, do not make the published world narrow too.
