# 覚えていないのに、知っている

## If I know who to ask, do I already “know” it?

Sometimes I cannot answer a question at all, yet I feel strangely confident.

「その契約の経緯は分からない。でも、Aさんに聞けば一発で分かる」

I do not know the answer.

But I know the address of the answer.

So am I ignorant, or do I know something after all?

Psychology has a concept aimed almost exactly at this strange middle zone: **transactive memory**.

<figure>
  <img src="https://silovar-uk.github.io/myessays/assets/transactive-memory/tms-johnson-quote.svg" alt="A quote card showing Samuel Johnson's idea that knowledge includes knowing a subject and knowing where to find it" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Wegner et al. opened their 1991 paper with Samuel Johnson's line, “Knowledge is of two kinds...”. Source: <a href="https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/dwegner/files/wegnererberraymond1991.pdf">Wegner, Erber & Raymond (1991)</a></figcaption>
</figure>

ざっくり言えば、it is a system for remembering **who knows what**, then reaching that person's memory when needed.

At first, I thought: 「要するに、記憶の外注やな」

That was half right and half much too simple.

## 1. Memory can store an address, not only content

The idea grew from Daniel M. Wegner and colleagues' 1985 work on **cognitive interdependence** in close relationships. Wegner later developed it in 1987 as a modern way to think about the old idea of a “group mind.”

This is not a mystical giant brain floating above a team.

Each person stores information internally. At the same time, each person can store a kind of **directory**: knowledge about who is likely to know a certain thing.

Imagine two people living together. One knows insurance and contracts; the other knows appliances and repairs.

The insurance person does not need to memorize every model number. 「それは相手が詳しい」と分かっていれば、必要なときに聞ける。

The appliance person can do the same in the opposite direction.

So the system is more than two memory capacities added together. **It also contains retrieval routes: who should I go to for this?**

<figure>
  <img src="https://silovar-uk.github.io/myessays/assets/transactive-memory/tms-directory-map.svg" alt="Concept diagram of transactive memory showing a person remembering routes to people who know contracts, numbers, and historical context" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>A minimal picture of transactive memory: not only information, but an index of who knows it. Concept source: <a href="https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/wjh/tm.htm">Daniel M. Wegner's Transactive Memory page</a></figcaption>
</figure>

ここが妙に面白い。

We usually imagine memory as content stored inside one brain.

But in a transactive memory system, **part of the usable memory is built into the structure of relationships.**

## 2. Tell a couple “you remember food,” and the system can get worse

A wonderfully concrete test came from a 1991 study by Wegner, Ralph Erber, and Paula Raymond.

The study involved 118 people who had been dating for at least three months. Some worked with their real partner; others were paired with an opposite-sex member of another couple.

Then the researchers manipulated memory responsibility. In some pairs, one person was assigned categories such as food while the other got categories such as history. Other pairs received no such structure.

しかも、学習中はペア同士で会話できない。

That is a very odd date.

And the result is the best part.

**Without an assigned structure, natural couples showed better memory performance than impromptu pairs. When the researchers imposed a memory structure, that relationship reversed.**

<figure>
  <img src="https://silovar-uk.github.io/myessays/assets/transactive-memory/tms-couples-experiment.svg" alt="A qualitative 2 by 2 diagram summarizing the 1991 natural-couple versus impromptu-pair memory experiment with and without assigned expertise" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>This is a qualitative diagram of the group relationship reported in the abstract, not a reconstruction of numerical data. Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1774630/">Wegner, Erber & Raymond (1991)</a></figcaption>
</figure>

The interpretation is striking.

People who already know each other may have an implicit division of memory: 「これは自分、これは相手」.

If a researcher suddenly installs a different shelf system — “from today, you are the food person” — it can collide with the directory the pair already built.

So established pairs are not merely better because they can communicate more.

**They may have learned who should remember what.**

人間関係、思ったより設定ファイルが多い。

## 3. A strong team does not necessarily mean everybody knows everything

Move this idea into an organization and it is tempting to shout, 「情報共有を徹底しよう」.

But transactive memory is interesting partly because it points in the opposite direction.

**Everyone holding the same knowledge is not the same thing as having a strong transactive memory system.**

A shared team mental model emphasizes common understanding of goals, strategies, tasks, and the working environment.

TMS assumes that knowledge can be **differentiated**.

A knows law. B knows the client history. C knows data.

The point is not to make A memorize as much analytics as C. The point is to know, **“data goes to C; this edge case also needs B.”**

全員を同じ百科事典にするのではなく、互いにリンクされた専門書の棚をつくる。

That is much closer to the TMS picture.

## 4. How do you measure a “team memory” without turning it into vibes?

The concept is elegant. But 「うちのチーム、TMS高そう」 is not much of a measurement strategy.

So in 2003, Kyle Lewis developed a 15-item field scale and validated it across 124 laboratory teams, 64 MBA consulting teams, and 27 technology-company teams.

Three dimensions became central.

- **Specialization（専門化）**：different members hold distinct expertise
- **Credibility（信頼性）**：members trust one another's knowledge
- **Coordination（協調）**：the team can combine and retrieve that knowledge smoothly

<figure>
  <img src="https://silovar-uk.github.io/myessays/assets/transactive-memory/tms-three-components.svg" alt="Triangle diagram of the three dimensions associated with a transactive memory system: specialization, credibility, and coordination" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption>Three manifestations emphasized in Lewis's measure. Source: <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12940401/">Lewis (2003)</a></figcaption>
</figure>

ここで「外付け脳」という比喩が少し壊れる。

An external SSD usually works when you plug it in.

Humans do not.

Someone may possess expert knowledge, but the team will not use it if others do not trust that person's expertise. Trust is not enough either if the group cannot coordinate access at the right moment.

**TMS includes not only where knowledge lives, but the relationship that makes retrieval possible.**

So “outsourced memory” is too weak. It looks more like **a protocol for operating distributed memory**.

## 5. Delete the org chart and draw only “who knows what”

ここで一度、調べすぎた人間らしく変なことをする。

Take an organization chart and erase every formal title.

部長、課長、担当、プロジェクトリーダー。いったん忘れる。

Next to each person, write only things like these.

「2019年の意思決定の経緯を知っている」

「この取引先が嫌がる言い回しを知っている」

「数字の定義が途中で変わった理由を知っている」

「このシステムが壊れたときの復旧手順を知っている」

A different map appears.

And in many workplaces, you find someone whom everybody asks first, almost automatically.

That person may not simply have too many tasks. **They may be functioning as the team's search engine.**

ここで「属人化」という言葉も少し細かく見える。

The problem is not only that one person holds unique content.

**The whole group may depend on a retrieval route that passes through that person.**

## 6. Is Google a colleague?

At this point, Google becomes impossible to ignore.

In 2011, Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu, and Daniel Wegner published four experiments in *Science* on internet access and memory.

One result: when people expected to have future access to information, they recalled the information itself less well but were better at remembering **where it could be accessed**.

The paper described the Internet as a major form of external or transactive memory.

かなり現代っぽい。

「覚えなくても検索すればいい」は、単なる怠慢ではなく、memory allocationとして観測できる。

But we should not stretch the term carelessly.

The original TMS concept grew as a **social system** in which people learn one another's expertise, trust it, and coordinate retrieval.

Google does not learn that I am 「サッカー史に詳しい同僚」 and build reciprocal trust with me in the same interpersonal sense.

So it is safer to say this: **the principle of remembering “where” rather than “what” also appears in digital environments**, even if a search engine is not simply identical to a human TMS partner.

## 7. “TMS makes teams better” is where the research starts yelling at us

ここまで読むと、TMSを導入すればチームが強くなるように見える。

Stop there.

A 2019 meta-analysis combined 76 empirical studies representing 6,869 sampling units and found that the TMS–performance relationship **varied with context**.

Environmental volatility, leadership effectiveness, team human capital and other factors were associated with TMS development, and cultural context changed the strength of the TMS–performance link.

So “know who knows what and you win” is not a universal cheat code.

A 2026 longitudinal study of 167 athletes across 15 field-hockey teams found that pre-tournament TMS predicted teamwork behaviors during the tournament and was linked to overall team performance.

スポーツでも、単に全員が同じ戦術を暗記するだけではなく、**who handles what, and whose judgment gets used**, という認知的な分業が効いている可能性が見える。

Meanwhile, a recent study of hospital trauma teams emphasizes that membership churn can work against TMS development.

A strong TMS is useful, but when people change, the directory needs updating.

名簿を変えただけでは、チームの頭は差し替わらない。

## 8. In a handover, is “knowledge” really the only thing that leaves?

Now for an extreme thought experiment.

B leaves a team.

Every file B owned goes into Notion. There are minutes, manuals, searchable archives. 完璧に見える。

But the remaining members also stored things like this in their own heads.

「この件ならBさん」

「この数字はBさんなら違和感に気づく」

「AさんとCさんの意見が割れたら、Bさんに背景を聞く」

When B leaves, more than B's files disappear.

**All those links to B, stored in other people's heads, suddenly become dead links.**

Research has repeatedly found that membership turnover can disrupt TMS. A 2018 experiment also showed that communication-network structure can change how turnover affects TMS and group performance.

ここで「引き継ぎ」の意味が変わる。

A handover should not transfer only the content of knowledge.

**Who takes ownership now? Do others recognize that person as the new expert? Can the team reach them without hesitation when the moment comes?**

In other words, the handover is not finished at file migration.

It ends when **the retrieval routes have been rewired**.

## 9. I thought this was a theory about “not having to remember”

Return to the first question.

「自分は答えを知らない。でも、誰に聞けばいいかは知っている」

Does that count as knowing?

Before reading the research, I saw transactive memory as an energy-saving trick: let somebody else remember, so I do not have to.

That is partly true. Specialization can reduce the need for everyone to store the same details.

But the actual system is more demanding.

Learn who knows what.

Learn whether their knowledge is credible.

Coordinate access so the right knowledge can be retrieved at the right time.

When people change, update the directory.

**You get permission to forget some content only by continuing to remember the map between people and knowledge.**

そう考えると、記憶は頭蓋骨の内側だけにあるものには見えなくなってくる。

At least the knowledge we can actually use in work and daily life is built from both what is in our own heads and the roads leading into other people's heads.

「あれはあの人に聞けばいい」は、知らないことの告白ではある。

It is also a surprisingly sophisticated way of remembering.

And perhaps the frightening part is not forgetting an answer.

**It is losing the road to the answer without noticing that the road is gone.**
