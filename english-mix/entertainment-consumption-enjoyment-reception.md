# 映画を見ても映画は減らない。それでも私たちは「消費」する
## Consumption is one layer, not the whole relationship｜経済・文化・受容から言葉をほどく

「音楽をconsumeする」「映画をconsumeする」「コンテンツをconsumeする」。意味は分かる。Industry reportsにもresearchにも日常会話にも出てくる。それでも、少しstrangeな手触りが残る。

Water decreases when I drink it. Electricity is used up. Time does not come back. But if I stream a movie once, the movie is not one unit smaller for the next person. If I play a song one hundred times, the 101st play is not thinner.

So my first hypothesis was simple: maybe 「享受」 is better than 「消費」. We do not use a work up; we receive it and experience its value. That sounded much closer.

But dictionaries, economics, cultural policy, reception studies, and fandom research changed the picture. The problem was not simply a wrong word. It was that one market word was covering many different things a person actually does with entertainment.

---

## 1. HOOK｜A movie does not get lighter when you watch it

Try the easy cases first. We consume gasoline, electricity, physical energy, and time. In each case, using it strongly suggests that some remaining amount goes down.

Now say, “I consume a movie.” The physical meter disappears. A cinema seat and two hours of my life are scarce, yes. But the work itself still exists after I finish it. With digital distribution, another person may even access the same content at the same time.

That mismatch is the starting point. But “therefore consumption is a misuse” would be too quick.

## 2. FACT｜The word really does carry a “use it up” meaning

Japanese dictionaries support the feeling. One major dictionary gives the first sense of 「消費」 as using something until it is gone, while another sense is economic use of goods to satisfy wants. [コトバンク「消費」](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%B6%88%E8%B2%BB-79739)

So one word contains two frames: an everyday image of depletion and an economic image of final use. Entertainment feels odd because both frames can arrive in our head at once.

The word is not wrong. We may be using the economic sense, while the everyday “it disappears” sense keeps leaking into the sentence.

## 3. FACT｜In economics, consumption does not require literal disappearance

In the System of National Accounts, consumption goods and services are defined around direct satisfaction of needs or wants. The key analytical idea is use for needs and wants, not a requirement that the object literally vanish. [SNA 2008, Chapter 9](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/book/9789211615227/ch009.xml)

Cultural economics also uses “consumption of cultural goods and services” as a normal term. Ruth Towse treats buyers, audiences, visitors, and viewers as consumers when analyzing the demand side, while participation adds information that price and income alone cannot capture. [A Textbook of Cultural Economics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/textbook-of-cultural-economics/consumption-of-cultural-goods-and-services/50423A1370D775BEA19360B010D9B251)

Here, “consumer” is not a full description of a human being. It is a role inside a market model. If the question is who buys, how much they use, or how demand moves with price, the word is useful and precise.

## 4. RESEARCH｜Digital works can be goods that do not run out

Why is the clash especially visible in entertainment? Digital goods give one answer. Economist Danny Quah describes digital goods as non-rival: one person’s use does not normally reduce another person’s ability to use the same informational good. [Digital Goods and the New Economy](https://cepr.org/publications/dp3846)

In another paper, Quah points to software and video entertainment as consumption increasingly moving toward knowledge-like goods where material physicality matters less and non-rivalry matters more. [Technology Dissemination and Economic Growth](https://cepr.org/publications/dp3207)

This does not mean entertainment has no scarcity. Live seats, performers’ time, servers, licenses, limited goods, and our own attention are scarce. The non-rival point mainly applies to the informational content layer of a work.

That is exactly the interesting part. Economically, consumption is happening, while the informational work is not being used up. Entertainment is a place where everyday intuition and economic definition can drift far apart.

## 5. TEST｜Put six different words on the same entertainment

So take one simple act—watching a film—and rename it six ways. Each word reveals a different layer.

- 消費する: shows the market relation—purchase, use, demand, spending.
- 享受する: shows receiving and enjoying value from the work.
- 鑑賞する: shows attentive engagement with expression and aesthetic value.
- 受容する: shows interpretation—how the work is taken into one’s own context.
- 体験する: shows the whole event, including emotion, body, place, and other people.
- 参加する: shows acting back on the work or community and helping shape the relation.

These are not synonyms. They answer different questions. At this point, my first idea—replace 消費 with 享受—started to look too simple.

## 6. FINDING｜享受 is close, but it is not universal

「享受」 means receiving something, taking it in, and enjoying its benefit. It describes the relation as receiving value rather than depleting a resource. In that sense, my first instinct was not wrong. [コトバンク「享受」](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E4%BA%AB%E5%8F%97-478286)

But 享受 leans toward benefit and enjoyment. A film you hated, a disturbing documentary, or a mandatory training video does not always feel naturally described as something you “enjoyed” or “benefited from.” Another part of reality drops out.

享受 is also strong in the direction from work to audience, but weaker in the reverse direction. Playing a game, singing at a live show, writing analysis, making fan works, recommending a title—here the audience is no longer only receiving.

## 7. FACT｜Japanese cultural law places viewing, participation, and creation under a wider right

A Japanese law gave a useful clue. The Cultural Arts Basic Act speaks of people creating and 「享受」ing culture and the arts as part of living in a cultural environment. [文化芸術基本法](https://www.bunka.go.jp/seisaku/bunka_gyosei/shokan_horei/kihon/geijutsu_shinko/kihonho_kaisei.html)

The same law then separates concrete modes: people should be able to view or appreciate culture, participate in it, or create it. That distinction is useful—享受 can be a broad value-level concept, while 鑑賞, participation, and creation describe different forms of action.

This changed the framing. 享受 is not simply a more elegant synonym for consumption. It is especially strong when talking about access to culture and the ability to receive its value.

## 8. CONNECTION｜Audiences do not simply receive fixed meaning

Another useful word is 「受容」. A Japanese dictionary gives an arts-related sense: taking a work into one’s sensibility and engaging with it as a basis of appreciation. [コトバンク「受容」](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%8F%97%E5%AE%B9-529419)

Reception research makes a similar point. Kim Schrøder argues that actual audience readings are multidimensional, involving motivation, comprehension, evaluation, position, and more. [Making sense of audience discourses](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/136754940000300205)

Watching the same film does not copy the same meaning into every mind. People bring experience, knowledge, and position. Receiving a work also means reconstructing meaning.

## 9. EXTREME TEST｜Games, live events, and fandom make “consumer” feel thin

For someone quietly sitting in a cinema, “consumer” may still capture a useful slice. Now move to cases where the audience acts back.

- Games: choices and inputs are required for the work to unfold.
- Live events: audience response and singing can change the atmosphere of that specific performance.
- Sports: cheering, chants, displays, and travel help create the matchday environment.
- Fandom: reviews, analysis, fan works, translation, and community work expand the culture around a title.

Henry Jenkins and colleagues describe participatory cultures in which people join communities, produce creative work, collaborate, and shape the flow of media. [Confronting the Challenges of Participatory Culture](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262513623/confronting-the-challenges-of-participatory-culture/)

H.K. Lee’s study of anime fansubbing shows fans voluntarily translating and distributing cultural products as participatory media fandom. Economically they may still be consumers, but “consumer” alone does not describe what they are doing. [Participatory media fandom](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/participatory-media-fandom-a-case-study-of-anime-fansubbing/)

But the opposite exaggeration is also wrong: not every audience member is a co-creator. People are free to simply watch. The point is not to romanticize participation; it is to choose a word that matches the action.

## 10. INTERPRETATION｜The discomfort comes when a market role becomes the whole person

From here, this is my interpretation of the evidence rather than a dictionary or research fact.

“Consumer” is an excellent lens for markets. People buy tickets, subscribe to services, purchase goods, and spend usage time. If the business question is demand, there is no reason to ban the term.

But the lens becomes thin when it tries to explain the whole relation between a person and a work. They cried, misread it, argued with a friend, connected it to their life, supported it, remade it, or changed behavior the next day. Those events live on another layer from purchases and views.

So maybe I was not rejecting the word 消費 itself. I was reacting to the moment when one market role became the name for everything a person does with a work.

## 11. PROPOSAL｜Do not ban “consumer”; choose the word by the layer you mean

For practical writing, forcing one universal noun is the mistake. Choose the word based on the question.

- Market, revenue, and demand: 消費, consumer, buyer, user.
- Concrete behavior: audience, viewer, reader, listener, player.
- Access to culture and receiving its value: 享受.
- Meaning-making: 受容, interpretation.
- Helping shape a scene or culture: participation, fandom, community.

For example, “increase content consumption among young people” can be fine if the KPI is views or paid use. But when the topic is cultural access as a right or public value, “opportunities to 享受 culture and the arts” describes the aim more clearly.

The reverse also matters. “The amount of film 享受 rose 12%” is vague. If measurement is the goal, name the measurable behavior: views, purchases, attendance, or usage time.

## 12. DISCOVERY｜The work may not shrink, but the audience can change

Before researching, I thought the answer was mostly stylistic: 消費 feels cold, so replace it with 享受.

Now I think differently. 消費 is not wrong. 享受 can also be right. They simply cut reality along different lines.

A movie does not shrink when I watch it. But two hours of my life are spent. The market records a use. My mind rebuilds meaning. A recommendation changes circulation. Fan activity may add new culture around the work.

Trying to make one verb describe all of that was the real problem.

So when I see the word “consumer,” I want to ask one more question: what is this person actually doing here—buying, watching, receiving value, interpreting, or participating?

Entertainment can be consumed. But consumption is not everything people do with entertainment.

## APPENDIX｜Prompt for seriously investigating a word that feels wrong

Here is the reusable research prompt built from this investigation.

~~~text
You are a researcher and editor investigating why a word feels wrong.

Input
【Word】{{word}}
【Domain】{{domain}}
【Discomfort】{{why it feels strange}}
【Possible replacement】{{if any}}

Goal
Do not decide too early that the word is correct or incorrect.
Find:
1. where the word is precise,
2. where it drops important information,
3. what each alternative reveals and hides,
4. whether one replacement is enough or different roles need different words.

Process
1. Start with one extreme concrete example where the discomfort is strongest.
2. Check dictionaries and separate everyday meaning from specialist meaning.
3. Read primary sources, law or policy, peer-reviewed research, and specialist books.
4. Identify contexts where the original word works well before criticizing it.
5. Generate at least five alternatives and compare the relation each one describes.
6. Stress-test them with digital goods, participation, negative experiences, and free use.
7. Re-research specifically for evidence that breaks the first hypothesis.
8. Separate fact, interpretation, and proposal.
9. End with a practical map: who is doing what, in which relationship, and which word fits.
10. State how the view changed from before research to after research.

Quality rules
- Do not stop at dictionary definitions.
- Do not let etymology dictate present meaning.
- Do not call a specialist use “wrong” only because everyday intuition differs.
- Do not treat an alternative as a universal solution.
- Include source URLs.
- Include the strongest counterexample.
- Aim for precision by context, not word policing.

Output
A. Initial discomfort
B. Facts found
C. Counterevidence that changed the hypothesis
D. Alternative-word comparison
E. Practical usage map
F. Before → after shift
G. Sources
~~~
