# 時計の中に、時間は入っていない

## Bergson meets the atomic clock, and suddenly “one second” looks strange.

I searched a very ordinary question — **How do we measure time?** — and NIST immediately gave me a slightly unsettling answer.

Strictly speaking, NIST says, what we measure is not “time itself” as a physical substance, but **time intervals between events**.

I went looking for metrology and somehow walked into philosophy.

So what is actually inside a clock?

Gears. A pendulum. Quartz. Electronics. Cesium atoms. Lasers. Detectors. Plenty of things.

But there is no physical object called **one second** sitting in the case.

That led me to a simple hypothesis.

**Maybe humans cannot feel time itself. Maybe we only infer it from changes in matter.**

It sounded vaguely Bergsonian.

Then I actually read *Time and Free Will*, and the idea flipped.

**Bergson’s distinction is not “unfelt time” versus “time inferred from change.” It is measurable, homogeneous time versus lived duration.**

## 1. Open a clock, and you find a cycle plus a counter

NIST gives a wonderfully unromantic definition: a clock needs a regular repeating process and a way to count those repetitions and display the result.

A pendulum clock counts swings.

A quartz clock counts crystal vibrations. A common watch oscillator runs at 32,768 Hz, then electronics divide that frequency down into one-second ticks.

Even an atomic clock does not suddenly capture a substance called time. It locks electromagnetic radiation to the cesium-133 resonance and counts cycles.

The SI second is defined by fixing the cesium-133 hyperfine transition frequency at exactly **9,192,631,770 Hz**.

Greater precision does not eliminate change. We simply found more stable repeating processes.

**At the mechanism level, a clock looks less like a device that extracts time and more like a device that counts repetition.**

But this is exactly where a catchy sentence can become misleading.

In metrology, clocks absolutely do measure time intervals. Saying “clocks do not measure time” without qualification would be wrong.

The interesting part is operational: once you unpack the measurement, you find events, cycles, correspondences and counts.

## 2. Classify clocks by one question: what change do they count?

I pushed the idea too far and rewrote clock history using only one column: **what changes?**

- Sundial: the changing position of a shadow relative to the Sun
- Water clock: changing water level produced by a regulated flow
- Pendulum clock: periodic swings
- Quartz clock: periodic crystal vibration
- Cesium atomic clock: cycles of radiation locked to an atomic transition

NIST describes the history of timekeeping in almost the same way: a search for increasingly consistent actions or processes to regulate a clock.

This is the odd part: clocks seem to search for something unchanging, but what they really need is something that **changes in the same way again and again**.

A perfectly motionless thing makes a terrible clock.

You need change — but disciplined change.

To measure time, we wait for something other than “time” to change.

## 3. If every change stopped, where would one second go?

Now for the deliberately excessive thought experiment.

Stop every clock in the world. Easy enough conceptually: clocks fail, but shadows still move, people still get hungry, hearts still beat.

Now stop not only clocks but shadows, water, pendulums, crystals, atomic states, bodies, memory — every possible change.

In that universe, what could you compare in order to say, “one second has passed”?

At minimum, no measuring device could answer. There would be no physical event distinguishing before from after.

That does **not** prove that time itself would cease to exist. At that point we have wandered into a much larger problem in physics and metaphysics.

But the thought experiment does reveal one modest thing.

**To measure an interval, we need distinguishable events.**

And that act of separating and arranging events is exactly where Bergson becomes interesting.

## 4. Bergson flips the original hypothesis

Bergson’s original *Time and Free Will* appeared in 1889. Its second chapter obsessively asks how countable time differs from lived duration.

He even asks what, exactly, is measured by the oscillations of a pendulum — almost suspiciously close to our question.

For Bergson, individual pendulum oscillations can be distinguished. Consciousness retains them and lays them out as one, two, three...

The flowing experience is turned into units that can be placed beside one another like points. This is the basic idea behind Bergson’s critique of the **spatialization of time**.

Clock seconds behave like equal boxes: second 1, second 2, second 3, side by side.

But is experience actually made of boxes?

Bergson says no. Conscious states do not simply disappear and get replaced by isolated successors; they interpenetrate, carrying the past forward as they change.

**Measured time becomes a homogeneous scale. Lived time — durée — continues as qualitative change.**

This is where my first hypothesis broke.

**A more Bergsonian claim is almost the reverse: we do experience time directly, but lived time does not have the same form as homogeneous clock time.**

External changes are not simply substitutes for an otherwise unfeelable time. They become handles for converting duration into countable units.

## 5. Three minutes can be equal and still not be the same

Daily life explains this faster than philosophy jargon.

Three minutes staring at your phone while waiting for a reply.

Three minutes inside a fascinating video that seems to vanish instantly.

On a clock, both are exactly 180 seconds.

As experiences, they are not the same shape.

Reducing Bergson to “boredom makes time feel longer” misses the point. His deeper claim is that the present of consciousness moves forward while carrying its past with it.

He uses examples involving sequences of sounds. If each sound were completely isolated, we would have separate noises, not a melody unfolding as a whole.

A note can be the **next** note only because what came before is somehow retained.

Duration is not an empty container receiving one second after another. It is closer to the whole experience changing character as it continues.

## 6. And yet clock time is unbelievably useful

At this point it is tempting to ask whether clock time is somehow fake.

That would be the wrong takeaway.

Homogeneous time is powerful precisely because it throws away qualitative differences.

You and I do not need to feel equally bored or equally happy at 10:00 in order to meet at 10:00.

As NIST notes, atomic clocks underpin synchronization for GPS, communications, computer networks and financial transactions.

Clock time does not need to invalidate private, qualitative experience. It also acts as infrastructure for putting events onto a shared coordinate system.

**A clock does not so much manufacture time as count change and translate it into shareable time.**

That sentence is my synthesis, not a quotation from Bergson.

## 7. This does not mean physics got time wrong

Bergson later developed his philosophy of time in relation to Einstein’s relativity, and the two debated directly in 1922. The episode remains controversial.

So durée should not be used as a one-line knockout argument against modern physics.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes a later Bergson letter in which he describes relativity as a major advance for both physics and philosophy.

This essay is not settling whether physical time “really exists.”

The question is smaller.

**Should measured clock time and lived time be treated as if they had exactly the same structure?**

Bergson becomes much clearer if we read him as saying: no — first, separate those two jobs.

## 8. Now I see change whenever I look at a clock

Back to the opening question.

Where, inside a clock, is one second?

As an object, nowhere.

What we find are moving shadows, flowing water, swinging pendulums, vibrating quartz, atomic resonances and electromagnetic cycles — plus mechanisms that count them.

We assign units to those repetitions and use them to synchronize our actions.

Meanwhile, before we ever check a clock, waiting can stretch, absorption can compress, and yesterday already colors how today feels.

Before researching this, I thought: humans cannot feel time, so we use change as a substitute.

Afterward, the picture looks almost reversed.

**Perhaps what requires counted change is not time itself, but the shared scale on which we coordinate it.**

When a clock stops, the first thing that stops is the machine that makes the scale.

And before we look at that scale, we are already living through time.
