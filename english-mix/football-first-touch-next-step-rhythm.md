# サッカーが退屈なら、ゴールの代わりに右足を見る――Finding the next beat

Watching football only for the next goal can turn ninety minutes into a waiting room. No goal yet. Another sideways pass. Back to the defender. Still waiting.

では、watch the right foot instead.

Did the player receive with the inside of the right foot? Stop it under the sole? Move the ball toward the left foot for the next action? Did the opponent close down *before* or *after* that touch?

A scoreless minute is not necessarily an empty minute. そこにはsmall choices, reactions, and new possibilitiesが連続している。

Now consider an entirely different problem: **leaving home**. 「家を出る」は一つの動詞に見えるが、standing up, getting dressed, finding keys and putting on shoes are different actions. On a difficult morning, “leave the house” may be too big a command. “Pick up the right sock” is smaller.

Could both experiences be about the size of the **next move**? これが本稿の仮説。It is an interpretive connection, not a proven psychological law.

> **Research note — 10 October 2026.** This article separates evidence from interpretation and practical proposals. No experiment reported here has directly demonstrated that watching feet increases spectator enjoyment or that the proposed home routine improves departure rates.

## 1. 結果を待つと90分が長くなり、接触を追うと一秒が長くなる / Change the unit of attention

The same match offers several **units of observation**. If your unit is the goal, a large stretch of play can feel like a pause. If the unit is a touch, an apparently ordinary pass turns into a sequence: preparation, contact, reaction, and the next option.

Imagine a midfielder receiving with the right foot, moving the ball left, and passing forward. From a result-only view: **one completed pass**. From a process view: Why the right foot? Why move the ball left? What became available after the second contact?

これは実試合の分析ではなく、架空の場面だ。The point is not that we can objectively create more events. We can ask **more discriminating questions** of what is already happening.

Boredom is not always a property of the game. Sometimes it is also a property of what we choose to count as an event.

## 2. ボールへの最初の接触には、次の一手の設計が隠れている / First touch changes possibilities

In football coaching, a **first touch** is not simply the moment the ball stops. It can set up the next pass, dribble, or turn.

FIFA's training materials explain how **body orientation** affects whether a teammate passes toward the receiver's left or right foot, to their feet, or into their path. Other FIFA exercises emphasize moving the ball into a position that opens a useful passing angle.

だから「右足で触った」はthe beginning of a question, not the end of an answer. Did that touch open a passing lane? Did it protect the ball? Did it reduce the player's options?

There is no universal rule that the right foot is better than the left. Speed, pressure, preferred foot, body shape, and intended next action all matter. A better question than “Which foot?” is **“What did this touch make possible next?”**

Sources: [FIFA — Directing the ball with the first touch](https://www.fifatrainingcentre.com/en/practice/elite-sessions/in-possession/directing-the-ball-with-the-first-touch.php) / [FIFA — Passing and receiving](https://www.fifatrainingcentre.com/en/practice/elite-sessions/in-possession/john-peacock-passing-and-receiving.php)

## 3. ところが上手な選手ほど、ボールばかり見ていない / Experts look beyond the ball

And here comes the inconvenient part. 足を見るのが面白いと言ったのに、research suggests that skilled players often look elsewhere.

In a 1994 video-based study, experienced players looked more at the positions and movements of other players than inexperienced players, who attended more often to the ball or passer. A 2022 Japanese study compared 24 skilled players and 22 novices during a receive-and-return task. Novices tended to watch the ball until just before trapping it; skilled players attended more to the intended receiver or passing space.

A 2020 study examined **27 Premier League players across 21 matches and 9,574 possessions**. Their exploratory head movements—**scanning**—varied with playing position, pressure, and action context.

But these studies concern **players' perception and performance**, not how spectators experience entertainment. And defensive one-on-one studies also show that information around the opponent's knees, hips, feet, and ball can matter. They do not imply that expert eyes never visit the ball.

観戦への示唆はむしろこうだ。Begin with the foot as an anchor. Then expand: What did the player look at before receiving? Who moved afterward? **The foot is a doorway to the whole play, not the whole play itself.**

Sources: [Williams et al., 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8047704/) / [Sakamoto and Ikeda, 2022](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/rjsp/14/0/14_2149/_article/-char/en) / [Jordet et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7573254/) / [Defensive visual search, 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15648495/)

## 4. リズムとは同じ速さで動くことではなく、次の瞬間を予想できることでもある / Rhythm without a metronome

Rhythm is often confused with speed. でもfootball is not a metronome. Players stop, delay, turn back, accelerate, and wait. The intervals are irregular.

Research on **temporal expectation** explains how humans anticipate *when* events may occur. A 2018 review by Anna C. Nobre and Freek van Ede describes several forms of temporal structure—rhythms, cues, event sequences, and other regularities—that can guide attention.

Here is the essay's interpretation, not a direct experimental finding: we can experience football as a chain of **expectation and revision**. Receive → defender approaches → ball is released → teammate changes position. Each action changes what we expect next.

だからbackward passes and pauses are not automatically “nothing.” They might draw an opponent out, or reveal that forward options are blocked. Knowing the difference requires context.

**Rhythm may include hesitation.** What matters is not only how fast the events arrive, but whether we can sense how one event leads into another.

Source: [Nobre & van Ede — Anticipated moments, 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn.2017.141)

## 5. やりすぎの机上実験――同じ三本のパスに、三種類の実況を付ける / A three-lens thought experiment

Let's go a little too far. 同じ架空の場面にthree kinds of commentaryを付ける。

A central midfielder receives a pass. An opponent approaches. The player passes backward, and another teammate plays sideways. Three passes, no goal.

- **Outcome commentary:** “Sideways. Backward. Sideways. Still no progress.”
- **Touch commentary:** “Received with the inside of the right foot. Moved it left. The next player played with the left foot.”
- **Before-and-after commentary:** “Checked the surroundings before receiving. Opened the body. The opponent blocked the inside lane, so the ball went back. A teammate then created a new angle.”

The third version adds imagined motives and movements. It is not evidence about an actual match or proof that the third option is the most enjoyable. What we have **actually performed here** is a textual comparison of different observation questions.

A real viewer test could compare several roughly three-minute, non-scoring sequences under these three instructions, recording enjoyment and perceived ability to predict the next action. Rewatching the same clip creates memory effects, so order and clips would need to be balanced. **That proposed spectator study has not been conducted here.**

The thought experiment cannot establish that detail causes enjoyment. It can show exactly how a vague instruction like “watch carefully” becomes a testable viewing method.

## 6. 「家を出る」は一つの動作ではない。だから止まりやすい / One word, many decisions

Now return to the front door. “Leave home” sounds like one task, but hides a chain: stand up, choose clothes, get dressed, find keys, put on shoes, open the door.

A vague goal compresses many decisions into one command. When an unexpected choice appears, the sequence may stall.

そこで「家を出る」を **“Pick up the right sock.”** に変える。Then the left sock. Then the shoes. Neither foot has magical motivational power. The advantage, if there is one, comes from **making the next action concrete**.

This is not a universal cure for difficulty initiating tasks. Fatigue, health, obstacles, motivation, and other circumstances matter. Micro-steps are an experiment with task design, not a diagnosis of why someone is stuck.

## 7. 研究が支えるのは「気合い」より、合図と行動を先につなぐ方法である / If–then plans

Psychology offers a relevant concept: **implementation intentions**, usually called **if–then plans**.

The structure is simple: **If situation X happens, then I will do action Y.** “When I put down my morning drink, I will go to the door and arrange my shoes.” This links a recognizable cue to a predetermined action.

Gollwitzer and Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis reported results from **94 independent tests**, with a medium-to-large average effect on goal attainment (**d = 0.65**). That does not mean a 65% improvement for each person. Nor does it prove that putting on the right sock first will make someone leave home.

大事なのはthe timing of the decision. Decide in advance where the action begins, so the starting moment does not require negotiating the same choice again.

The goal is not **never think**. It is **don't repeatedly make decisions that could already have been made**.

Sources: [Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1) / [Research overview of mechanisms, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4500900/)

## 8. ただし、一歩ずつ数え続けると、むしろ動けなくなることがある / Chunking beats micromanagement

Here is the reversal. 何でも細かくする、は最終目標ではない。

With practice, separate movements can become organized into a larger action unit. Researchers call this **action chunking**. Reviews of motor sequence learning discuss the role of cortico–basal ganglia circuits in forming such sequences. Much of that evidence comes from specialized laboratory and animal tasks, not ordinary morning routines.

A soccer-dribbling experiment with **10 skilled and 10 less-skilled players** offers a useful warning: instructions that directed skilled players to consciously monitor the feet or arms interfered with their performance.

So small instructions may help **start** an action, while constant monitoring may hinder an action already learned. The implications for viewers and performers are different, and should not be blurred.

Use “right foot, left foot” as temporary scaffolding. Once you're moving naturally, **stop counting**. Return to tiny steps when you get stuck, not because every movement must be supervised.

Sources: [Jin & Costa, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4523429/) / [Attention in a soccer-dribbling task, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16120565/)

## 9. 提案――リズムは「着火・接続・手放す」の三段階で設計する / Start, link, release

This is the article's own **practical proposal**, not a clinically tested method.

**Start — 着火.** Choose one familiar, obvious action. For leaving home: put both feet on the floor. For starting a report: open the file. The first step should be small enough not to require a new plan.

**Link — 接続.** Let a completed action cue the next. Open file → read first heading. Pick up a sock → put it on. Use visible objects as cues where helpful, so you do not need to repeatedly ask “Now what?”

**Release — 手放す.** When the sequence becomes natural, remove the instructions. You do not need to narrate how to put on shoes if your body already knows. Break things down again only when an obstacle appears.

This is not a race. Some decisions genuinely deserve attention. Others only consume time because we insist on deciding them again.

The aim is to make room for thought **by not spending thought everywhere**.

## 10. サッカーでも日常でも、見えないのは「次」が生まれる瞬間だった / The next step was there all along

Back to football. Looking at a right foot can change a sideways pass from “nothing happened” into a small chain of events. The player prepared, touched, adapted, and offered the next possibility.

But the research changed the original idea. Skilled players do not merely look harder at the ball. They scan the environment. And practiced movements do not remain forever divided into tiny instructions. They become larger units.

だからthe right foot is a starting point, not a permanent obsession.

On a hard morning, start with a sock. But once you're walking toward the door, there is no need to narrate “right, left, right, left.”

**Rhythm is not counting every move forever. It is helping the first move become a sequence that can continue without counting.**

The next time a midfielder plays an uneventful sideways pass, a new question remains: how many small decisions did that player no longer need to make consciously to produce such an ordinary-looking action?

## 調査メモ / Evidence, interpretation, proposal

**Evidence:** First touches and body orientation can shape subsequent play. Experienced football players gather information from different visual regions depending on context. If–then plans show an average positive effect on goal pursuit. Learned action sequences can become chunked; conscious internal monitoring can interfere with skilled dribbling.

**Interpretation:** Spectator attention and everyday action initiation can both be examined through the “size of the next move.” This is a conceptual bridge, not one scientifically established mechanism.

**Proposal:** Use three observation lenses when watching football, and “start → link → release” when beginning a task. Neither method's benefit has been established here by original experiments.
