# 「嘘を嘘と見抜け」だけでは、languageの半分しか見えていない
## 「今日は暑くない？」からRwandan genocideまで。speech act theoryは「言ったことでwhat changed?」を見る

「今日、暑くない？」と聞かれて、"Yeah, it is hot." と答える。As a statement about temperature, you are correct. You are not lying. なのに数秒後、「いや、エアコンつけてほしかったんだけど」と言われることがある。Your answer was true, and still slightly wrong.

この妙なfailureは、languageを"a box containing information"だと思うと説明しにくい。「今日は暑い」というcontentだけを採点すれば正解だからだ。But the other person may not have been sharing a weather observation; they may have been making an indirect request. Same sentence, different action.

ここから急にwarやhate speechへ飛ぶのはroughなので、慎重に進めたい。Still, one question survives the change in scale: **when people speak, what are they doing besides describing facts?** The philosopher who pushed this question hard in the twentieth century was ジョン・L・オースティン（J. L. Austin）.

そしてこのquestionを通すと、「嘘を嘘と見抜ける人でないと掲示板を使うのは難しい」という有名なlineも違って見える。It is not bad advice. In fact, it is necessary. But speech still has work left to do even after a truth check.

> This essay starts from ゆる言語学ラジオ「ひろゆきから始める言語哲学」and rechecks its claims against Austin, Lynne Tirrell, the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda（ICTR）, and empirical research. Where the video and the sources diverge, the sources take priority.

## 1. 「今日は暑くない？」may not be a report about temperature

日常会話をdictionary literallyに読むと、often it becomes weird. 「窓、開いてるよ」can report the state of a window, or function as "please close it." 「明日早いんだよね」can share a schedule, or signal "maybe you should go home soon." Every day, we read one layer outside sentence meaning almost automatically.

ここでpointは、「learn to read hidden intentions」という処世術ではない。The point is that the action performed by the same string can change with context. 「水をください」does not state a truth about water; it requests water. 「ごめんなさい」does not merely describe a past event; it apologizes. 「明日やります」can be a prediction, but in context it can become a promise.

すると、our unit of analysis changes. We no longer ask only whether a sentence "matches the world." We also ask, **what is the speaker trying to make happen by uttering it now?** A factually correct reply can miss the conversation because it answers the content but not the action.

小さなconversation failureが、ここでphilosophyの入口になる。Language talks about the world, but it also creates the next move between people. The moment a remark about temperature becomes an air-conditioner request, speech has stepped half a pace from description toward action.

[大阪大学大学院言語文化研究科「言語文化学事典」](https://www.lang.osaka-u.ac.jp/lc/research/enclc/) ／ [ゆる言語学ラジオ「ひろゆきから始める言語哲学」](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B4S9MY0BEgU)

## 2. Austin split one utterance into "what was said," "what was done in saying it," and "what happened by saying it"

オースティンのargument did not begin as a giant slogan like "words create reality." It began with a quieter observation: everyday life is full of utterances that are awkward to grade simply as true or false. Wedding declarations, naming, promising, apologizing, warning. Treating all of them as strange versions of factual reports feels too narrow.

そこでAustin separated several layers. In standard Japanese terminology, **発語行為（locutionary act）** is saying something meaningful; **発語内行為（illocutionary act）** is what one does in saying it, such as promising, ordering, or warning; **発語媒介行為（perlocutionary act）** concerns effects brought about by saying it, such as persuading, frightening, or moving someone to act.

たとえば"The bridge is dangerous." The content describes the bridge. In context, it may simultaneously warn, "do not cross." If the listener turns back, the utterance has affected behavior too. Description, action, and consequence overlap inside one short sentence.

ここで"language is not a tool for representing facts"と言い切ると、we become sloppy in the opposite direction. Austin did not delete factual description. **He stopped imprisoning language inside factual description alone.** What looked like a camera also had buttons and levers. That extra machinery is the most practical part of speech act theory.

[Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “John Langshaw Austin”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/austin-jl/) ／ [Oxford University Press, How To Do Things With Words](https://academic.oup.com/book/5162) ／ [講談社『言語と行為――いかにして言葉でものごとを行うか』](https://www.kodansha.co.jp/book/products/0000314385)

## 3. 「嘘を嘘と見抜け」is right; it just misses the second half of an utterance if truth is all we inspect

西村博之の「うそはうそであると見抜ける人でないと（掲示板を使うのは）難しい」は、2000年のtelevision interviewで広く知られるようになった。Anonymous boards mix truth and falsehood; users need judgment. Taken on its own, this still works perfectly well as a rule for social media in 2026.

Speech act theory gives us no reason to throw that away. Whether "a typhoon is coming tomorrow" is true matters. Whether "this medicine will definitely cure you" corresponds to reality matters. Truth checking does not disappear.

But an utterance does not become harmless the instant everyone detects the lie. A false claim that "this person is a criminal" can still contaminate search results, force the target to respond, and direct collective attention even if many people later reject it. A joke can remain "just a joke" and still teach a group who may be treated as the recurring target.

つまり「嘘を見抜け」はstrong as **content inspection**. Yet speech has processes left after inspection: who was pushed to do what, who was placed where, what became easier to say next. Speech act theory does not add an anti-intellectual "truth does not matter." It adds **an audit of effects and actions that truth conditions alone do not contain**.

## 4. In Rwanda, language did not invent ethnicity from zero; it helped transform existing boundaries into categories of killable enemies

ここからはjokesを止める。From April to July 1994, Rwanda experienced mass killing directed mainly at Tutsi people and also at Hutu who opposed the genocide. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum（USHMM）states that as many as one million people were killed in roughly one hundred days. Reducing this to "the danger of metaphor" would erase politics, war, armed organizations, administration, coercion, and local interests.

First, Hutu and Tutsi were not categories invented from nothing by Belgian colonialism. Social distinctions existed before colonial rule. But the boundaries had more mobility, while colonial rule added racialized explanations and hardened classifications through institutions such as ethnic identity cards. After independence, discrimination and violence continued; after the Rwandan Patriotic Front（RPF）invaded in 1990, extremists intensified propaganda linking domestic Tutsi to the rebel force.

One repeated label was **イニェンジ（inyenzi）**, whose everyday Kinyarwanda meaning is "cockroach." Its political origin is complicated. The term was used for armed Tutsi exile groups in the 1960s, but sources differ on whether it began as a self-designation or an external comparison to nocturnal insects. What is well documented is that in the 1990s its target expanded from RPF fighters toward Tutsi people more generally.

So the frightening process was not a magical word transforming a human into an insect. It was the repeated layering of pest, infiltrator, and enemy inferences onto a boundary that already existed politically. **Language did not simply draw the boundary; it changed what the boundary meant, moving it from neighbor-versus-neighbor toward enemy-versus-target.** Keeping that distinction prevents us from exaggerating language while underplaying institutions and organized responsibility.

Simon Bikindi judgment](https://unictr.irmct.org/files/case-documents/ictr-01-72/trial-judgements/en/081202.pdf)

## 5. "Cockroach" is not merely an insult; it can carry a compressed package of inferences about how a target may be treated

Philosopher リン・ティレル（Lynne Tirrell）, in “Genocidal Language Games,” does not treat derogatory labels as merely unpleasant names. When a term circulates repeatedly inside a society, she argues, the inferences and patterns of action attached to that term can begin to move with it.

Suppose we say "A is like a cockroach." There are obviously similarities and differences. Humans do not have antennae or live behind kitchen cabinets. But once the metaphor stabilizes, familiar assumptions from the second domain can slide across: cockroaches are dirty, multiply, invade, and should be exterminated. A short expression can transport many unspoken middle premises.

This becomes clearer if we think of metaphor as **a compressed file of inferences**, not simply a clever paraphrase. The filename is short. Unzip it and evaluations, predictions, permissions, and candidate actions appear. Tirrell's concern is therefore not only what a slur makes people believe about a target, but also what kinds of treatment it helps make seem permissible.

Of course, receiving the same compressed file does not make everyone perform the same action. Language, political authority, weapons, peer pressure, fear, and local social relations must converge. So "metaphor kills people" is too strong. A better claim is that **metaphor can lay inferential tracks that make previously unthinkable actions easier to think and easier to license**.

[Lynne Tirrell, “Genocidal Language Games”](https://academic.oup.com/book/34639/chapter-abstract/295132361) ／ [Lynne Tirrell, “Language and Genocide”](https://lynne-tirrell.uconn.edu/projects/) ／ [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Metaphor”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2025/entries/metaphor/)

## 6. Radio turned words from "one person's statement" into a coordination device: everyone else may be hearing the same thing

Derogatory labels were not the only issue in Rwanda. Radio Télévision Libre des Mille Collines（RTLM）and the newspaper Kangura spread anti-Tutsi propaganda. In the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda（ICTR）"Media Case," media leaders were prosecuted and convicted.

ここはvideoの説明を一つcorrectしたい。RTLM was not always speaking indirectly through phrases like "remove the pests." ICTR records include broadcasts that called on listeners to hunt Tutsi and alleged RPF accomplices, identified locations, and directly encouraged killing. Long-running classification and derogation coexisted with explicit mobilization.

Economist デービッド・ヤナギザワ＝ドロット（David Yanagizawa-Drott）then gives us another angle. He used differences in RTLM radio reception caused by Rwanda's mountainous terrain to estimate effects on participation in violence. His 2014 paper attributed an estimated 51,000 perpetrators, around ten percent of total participation, to the station's broadcasts and reported spillovers beyond directly receiving villages.

At that point radio is no longer merely a machine for sending false information to many individuals. Listeners can infer not only what they are being told to do but also that **other people are hearing the same instruction**. Shared speech can synchronize expectations about what everyone else may do next. The power of propaganda can sit not only in content but in coordinating the next social move.

[ICTR, “Three Media Leaders convicted for Genocide”](https://unictr.irmct.org/en/news/three-media-leaders-convicted-genocide) ／ [David Yanagizawa-Drott, “Propaganda and Conflict”](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/propaganda-and-conflict-evidence-rwandan-genocide)

## 7. Then the video's "most soldiers did not fire" claim sends us straight back to fact-checking

ここまで読むと、the message may sound like "look beyond truth and focus on what language does." Conveniently, the video itself gives us a trap. Early on it repeats the famous claim that most soldiers in World War II did not fire at the enemy. This comes from S・L・A・マーシャル（S. L. A. Marshall）, who reported firing rates around fifteen to twenty-five percent.

The problem is that this number is not a settled fact. The U.S. Army Center of Military History itself describes Marshall's twenty-five-percent firing ratio as controversial, and later critics have challenged his interview methods, records, and reproducibility. A broad claim that humans may experience resistance to killing must be separated from the precise historical claim about what percentage of soldiers fired.

This is wonderfully inconvenient. While explaining that words are more than truth conditions, we suddenly have to stop and check whether the source video is factually right. But that inconvenience improves the argument. Speech act theory does not reduce the need to test what an utterance says by even one millimeter.

戻ってみると、「嘘を嘘と見抜け」のposition changes. It is not obsolete. **It is still gate one; there is simply a gate two behind it.** First ask whether the claim is true. Then ask what the utterance did. Philosophy of language does not replace fact-checking; it extends the inspection downstream.

[U.S. Army Center of Military History, “SLAM: The Influence of S. L. A. Marshall on the U.S. Army”](https://history.army.mil/Publications/Publications-Catalog/SLAM/)

## 8. Responsibility for words becomes clearer when we ask two questions: "Is it true?" and "What did saying it do?"

このtwo-axis view changes how common escape hatches on social media look: "this is only my personal opinion," "it was a joke," "I only said what I think." Those phrases may matter for classifying an utterance and its responsibility. But adding them does not automatically erase whether the earlier speech recommended, insulted, frightened, or normalized a way of treating someone.

The reverse warning matters too. If "speech is action" becomes a universal card, any disliked statement can be inflated into "it changed reality." Illocutionary force depends on context, authority, relations, conventions; perlocutionary effects vary too. Repetition and institutional support matter. That is why we should specify what action was actually performed instead of merely praising the mysterious power of words.

「今日は暑くない？」に「暑いね」と返した人は、not lying. Yet they may still fail to answer the request. Keep that small mismatch in view and scale it up, and falsehoods, advertisements, slurs, and propaganda can be examined with the same pair of questions: **Is it true? And what are we doing by saying it?**

Before researching this, speech act theory looked like a dramatic thesis: "words have power to change the world." After researching it, the theory looks almost annoyingly precise. Which words, in which situation, spoken with whose authority, opened which inferences and enabled which next move? Words change the world not by magic, but because **speaking is itself one of the things people actually do inside the world**.
