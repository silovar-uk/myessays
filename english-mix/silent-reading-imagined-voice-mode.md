# Silent reading is not only about scanning; you can also「声を立ち上げて」read
## Switch among scanning, meaning, and imagined voice by changing the question—not only the speed

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
「もういい」

Read these four Japanese characters silently.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Was the speaker angry? Resigned? Calmly satisfied? The letters themselves hardly tell you. それでも読む瞬間、some readers may automatically give the phrase a tone of voice. Others may understand it without experiencing anything that feels like a voice at all.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
That is the first strange thing about silent reading. From the outside, everything looks the same: closed mouth, no audible sound. 内側では、however, you may be searching for a keyword, following an argument, or mentally staging somebody's voice. One word—「読む」—covers several different cognitive acts.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
I used to think about silent-reading technique mostly in the direction of scanning and skimming: move faster by selecting less. Research points to another adjustable dial on the opposite side. **Instead of reducing what the eyes process, you can add a voice inside the text.** Silent reading can change not only by becoming faster, but by changing what kind of information you allow into the representation.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Ruvanee P. Vilhauer, “Characteristics of inner reading voices,” 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582587/) ／ [Alexander Strukelj & Diederick C. Niehorster, “One page of text,” 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7198234/)

> **Information cutoff: September 22, 2026**
>
> This essay does not claim that imagining a voice is universal or that it automatically improves comprehension. 研究で確認されているinner speech、auditory imagery、implicit prosodyを手がかりに、practical reading modesとしてどう切り替えられるかを考える。

## 1. Silent reading is not one speed; it already changes shape according to purpose

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
There is no need to make fast reading the villain. What matters is what you are trying to get from the page.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
A 2018 eye-tracking study compared regular reading, thorough reading, skimming, and spell checking. During skimming, readers made longer saccades, shorter fixations, skipped more words, and finished faster. その代わり、comprehension scores were lower than in regular or thorough reading. Thorough reading involved more rereading and better comprehension.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
So reading speed is not a simple ability meter. If your goal is to locate a name, date, or keyword, reading every sentence at equal density can be inefficient. 逆に、論理や細部を理解したいのにsearch modeのまま進めば、processing becomes shallower. Reviews of speed reading likewise emphasize a speed–accuracy trade-off.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
If we reduce the issue to two choices, we get “read fast” versus “read carefully.” But before speed comes a different variable: **what question are you asking the text?** Scanning asks, “Where is the thing I need?” The faster eye movement is a consequence of that question.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Strukelj & Niehorster, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33828678/) ／ [Keith Rayner et al., “So Much to Read, So Little Time,” 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26769745/)

## 2. The「voice」inside silent reading can carry your own accent

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Can imagined voice really count as part of reading? The research gets surprisingly specific here.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
In 2011, Ruth Filik and Emma Barber asked Northern and Southern English speakers to silently read limericks containing words whose pronunciation differs by regional accent. Whether a line rhymed therefore depended on the reader's own accent. When the rhyme failed according to that accent, eye-movement disruption appeared.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
There was no audio file on the page. Yet written words seem to have been recoded into the reader's own pronunciation strongly enough for rhyme to matter. 黙読は、文字から意味へstraight lineで飛ぶだけではなく、少なくとも一部の場面ではphonological representationを通る。

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
This makes the “inner voice” less metaphorical. **Silent reading can carry not only words but traces of how the reader would actually say them.** Even with the mouth closed, the reader's history of speaking can leak into the text.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Ruth Filik & Emma Barber, “Inner Speech during Silent Reading Reflects the Reader's Regional Accent,” 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3198452/)

## 3. Stranger still: imagining who is speaking can change reading speed

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
A voice is not only pronunciation. It belongs to a speaker.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
In 2008, Jessica D. Alexander and Lynne C. Nygaard familiarized participants with a fast talker and a slow talker. Later, readers were told that a passage had been written by one of those people. Even during silent reading, passages attributed to the slow talker took longer to read. The authors interpreted this as evidence for talker-specific auditory imagery.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Another study compared direct and indirect speech with similar meaning. Silent reading of direct quotations produced stronger activity in voice-selective auditory-cortex regions than indirect speech. 「誰かが実際に言った言葉」という形式が、more vivid voice simulationを引き出している可能性がある。

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
This is not a romantic claim that imaginative readers are “better” readers. The useful point is more mechanical: the imagined speaker can reach into processing time itself. 遅いtalkerを頭に置けば、silent readingも遅くなる。A mental model of the speaker can change how quickly the text is traversed.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
If speed is the only metric, that looks like a loss. But another interpretation is available. **You are not merely slowing down; you are adding a layer called “who said this, and how?”** Scanning removes information to move faster. Voice simulation may add information and therefore move slower. They operate in opposite directions.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Jessica D. Alexander & Lynne C. Nygaard, “Reading voices and hearing text,” 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18377181/) ／ [Bo Yao, Pascal Belin & Christoph Scheepers, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21452944/)

## 4. Try the same sentence in three different modes

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Research alone does not make the practical distinction vivid. So here is a tiny paper exercise—not a scientific experiment, but a way to expose the control knobs.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Read this sentence:

> 彼女は「大丈夫」と言った。三秒後、同じ質問をもう一度した。

First, **scanning mode**. You pick up “大丈夫,” “三秒後,” and “同じ質問.” The question is what happened and where the key facts are.

Second, **meaning mode**. She says she is fine, but three seconds later repeats the question. 発言と行動のずれを取る。

Third, **imagined-voice mode**. Hear「大丈夫」as bright, flat, rushed, delayed, or slightly shaky.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
The third mode appears to add information that is not actually written. That is true. There is no correct tone available from the sentence alone. でも、その勝手なsimulationをすると逆に、「the text does not determine the speaker's state this precisely」と気づける。Imagining a voice can reveal not only one possible meaning but also the boundaries of what the text actually specifies.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
The three modes are not simply fast, medium, and slow. They ask different questions:

- Scanning: “Where is it?”
- Meaning: “What does it say?”
- Imagined voice: “How is it being said?”

Once separated this way, silent reading looks less like speed control and more like **switching the variable you are observing**.

## 5. Imagined voice becomes useful when「how it is said」is part of the meaning

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
There is no reason to add an inner actor to every paragraph you read. The mode makes more sense when voice itself carries information.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Dialogue in fiction, interviews, speeches, essays, letters, advertising copy—these forms often depend on pause, emphasis, hesitation, irony, and character. Research on implicit prosody suggests that silent readers generate internal patterns of stress, rhythm, and phrasing, and that these patterns can affect eye movements and even syntactic interpretation.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
By contrast, if you are searching a timetable for a departure time, a contract for「解約」, or a report for a proper noun, talker simulation contributes little to the task. 文章が「what is said」だけで足りるのか、それとも「how it is said」までmeaningの一部なのかで、mode choiceは変わる。

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
A practical rule follows: **choose the mode by the answer you need, not by the genre label.** Search when you need location. Read relations when you need logic. Bring up a voice when delivery itself carries information. Reading expertise may be less about always going deep and more about knowing when to change depth.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Jane Ashby & Charles Clifton Jr., “The prosodic property of lexical stress affects eye movements during silent reading,” 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1479854/) ／ [Mara Breen, “Empirical Investigations of the Role of Implicit Prosody in Sentence Processing,” 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lnc3.12061)

## 6. But research does not show that「hear a voice = understand better」for everyone

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
This is the point where a neat reading hack would be tempting. The evidence does not justify that leap.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
In Vilhauer's survey of 570 respondents, 80.7% reported sometimes or always experiencing an inner reading voice during silent reading, while 19.3% reported understanding written words without hearing such a voice. People who did report voices described differences in gender, accent, pitch, loudness, and emotional tone.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
That does not prove one universal mechanism. It is self-report, and the subjective meaning of “hearing” an inner voice varies. Also, inner speech, auditory imagery, and implicit prosody overlap without being identical. 「口を動かさない発話表象」「誰かの声を心内再生する感覚」「文にリズムや抑揚を与える処理」を全部一つの“脳内音読”にまとめると、important distinctions disappear.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
So imagined-voice reading is not the correct form of silent reading. **It is an optional channel that some readers can add when the text rewards it.** There is no reason to treat people without a vivid inner voice as deficient, and no reason for people with one to eliminate it automatically.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Ruvanee P. Vilhauer, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28582587/) ／ [Ben Alderson-Day & Charles Fernyhough, “Inner Speech,” 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4538954/)

## 7. Silent reading is not「reading with no sound」; it is reading without sending the sound outside

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Return to「もういい」. You can scan past it, understand its dictionary meaning, or hear a particular person say it in your head.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Real reading probably shifts among these modes continuously. You skim the beginning of a chapter, scan for the relevant section, slow down for an argument, then suddenly hear a character's line. 分からない一文だけ、頭の中でpauseを置き直すこともある。Silent reading is not one stable technique; it is a sequence of switches.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Before researching this, I saw scanning and imagined voice as a contrast between fast and slow reading. That was too simple. Scanning amplifies「where」. Voice simulation amplifies「how」. Both are selective ways of increasing the salience of one layer of the text.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
That changes the meaning of silent reading a little. **Silent reading is not reading without sound. It is reading without putting the sound into the room, while still choosing what—if anything—you want to hear inside the text.** Learning to read faster is useful. So is knowing when to invite the voice back in.
