# “A third party angrier than the victim becomes an aggressor” is a test of who owns the anger
## Reading Akira Kawashima's line as a self-check for when justice stops being centered on the victim

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
「被害者より怒っている第三者は加害者だ」. The sentence sticks. It is short, sharp, and slightly dangerous. If read too literally, it can sound like an emotional speed limit: once an outsider becomes angrier than the victim, that outsider is automatically wrong.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
The line came from Akira Kawashima of the comedy duo Kirin. In a May 2021 episode of『川島・山内のマンガ沼』, he was talking with Uoto, author of『チ。―地球の運動について―』, about the social position of「being a victim」. Kawashima said that, while watching social media, he had long thought:「被害者より怒っている第三者は加害者だ」.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
The useful reading is not simply to compare quantities of anger. A third party may speak louder than the harmed person, demand harsher punishment, or keep attacking after the harmed person wants the matter to end. At that point, the question changes. Is this person still supporting the victim? Or has the victim's experience become a license for the outsider's own attack?

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
The strength of the line is not「outsiders should never be angry」. It is this: **when you believe you are standing on the side of justice, ask again whose anger this has become.**

> This essay does not treat Kawashima's line as a law proven by psychology or social science. I first verify the context of the remark, then compare it with research on third-party anger. The studies below are not sources Kawashima is known to have used; they are later evidence brought in for this essay's analysis.

## 1. The line appeared inside Uoto's discussion of competing for the「victim position」

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
This was not an aphorism dropped from nowhere. Just before it, Uoto was explaining a discomfort about the social position of「being a victim」.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Asked how memorable lines in『チ。』were created, Uoto said ideas often came when encountering something he did not want to accept. As one example, he described a sense that people sometimes compete for the position of「the victim」: people who are truly harmed should be respected, but joining that position can also make it easier to speak with authority.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
Kawashima then said,「SNSを見ていて、僕もずっと思っていることがあるんです」and followed with the line. In that context, it reads less like「anger is bad」and more like discomfort with a shift: a victim's situation can become a platform for someone else's self-expression or attack.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Victim, offender, and third party are positions defined partly by distance from the event. Online, however, distance and confidence do not always move together. The victim may know enough to avoid certainty, while an outsider may know little enough to speak with certainty. Less information does not always produce softer language.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
Seen this way, the line becomes a warning about **moments when distance from an event makes it easier to turn people into simple roles**. Distance does not guarantee calm. Sometimes it makes simplification easier.

## 2. If we read it as「never be angrier than the victim」, the idea becomes too crude

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Still, the line breaks if we turn it into a universal rule. A third party being more visibly angry than the victim is not automatically abnormal or harmful.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Sometimes harmed people cannot protest. They may have less power, fear retaliation, be exhausted, or not yet have words for what happened. Sometimes the problem is larger than one person's case and requires changing a repeated pattern. In those situations, outsiders becoming angry, speaking up, and demanding reform can matter.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
Behavioral science has long studied「third-party punishment」: people sanctioning norm violations even when they were not directly harmed. Experiments by Ernst Fehr and Urs Fischbacher found that third parties were willing to pay a cost to punish unfairness that did not directly change their own payoff. Moral outrage has also been studied as an emotion that can support solidarity-based collective action.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
So outsider anger can help maintain society as well as damage it. It can enforce a norm and publicly mark a boundary:「this is not acceptable」. If every third-party reaction is called aggression, then whistleblower supporters, anti-discrimination allies, and people protesting institutional abuse all collapse into the same category.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
To keep Kawashima's line useful, we need to move from **「is the outsider angry?」to「what is the outsider's anger doing?」** Is it helping? Preventing repetition? Correcting information? Or is it using the victim as material for expanding the attack? The important difference is function, not temperature.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Fehr & Fischbacher, “Third-party punishment and social norms,” 2004](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1090-5138(04)00005-4) ／ [Green, “Solidarity-based collective action among third parties,” 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/asap.12368)

## 3. On social media, outrage can receive rewards that have little to do with the victim

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
The problem becomes harder because anger can be both a tool for support and a behavior that rewards the speaker.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Post strong moral anger online and reactions come back: likes, reposts, agreement, new followers, approval from your group. This does not mean most people are pretending. But「express outrage」and「receive social feedback」are connected on the same interface, so anger does not stay inside the relationship between victim and supporter.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
In a 2021『Science Advances』study, William Brady and colleagues analyzed about 7,300 Twitter users and 12.7 million tweets, alongside behavioral experiments. Positive social feedback for outrage expression increased the likelihood of future outrage expression. Users also adjusted their expression toward the norms of their social networks.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
This does not mean the first emotion was fake. Someone may genuinely care about the victim. But if outrage also returns approval, identity, and belonging, there are now extra reasons to keep performing it. Anger can continue on a circuit that no longer depends on whether the victim's situation is improving.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
That makes the aphorism more precise. **The risky moment may not be when an outsider becomes “too angry,” but when the anger can continue independently of the victim's condition.** If the fuel shifts from「I want this person helped」to「I need to remain the person who is angry」, the subject has changed.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Brady et al., “How social learning amplifies moral outrage expression in online social networks,” 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8363141/)

## 4. 「Saying the right thing」and「showing that I am right」can look almost identical

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
There is another complication: moral speech can affect the speaker's status as well as the issue being discussed.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Psychology and philosophy have studied「moral grandstanding」, broadly referring to the use of moral talk in pursuit of social status. This does not mean「everyone who speaks about justice is status-seeking」. The research asks whether status motives can mix into public moral discussion.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
A 2019 study by Joshua Grubbs and colleagues, using several samples, reported associations between moral-grandstanding motivation and greater political and moral conflict in daily life. But the research does not give us a tool for diagnosing the hidden intention behind any single post, and strong language alone is not evidence that someone is grandstanding.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Still, the concept fits the problem. Third-party anger can become risky without conscious bad faith. I may want confirmation that I am on the right side, recognition from my group, or proof that I am more ethical than people staying quiet. Those motives can coexist with real concern for the victim, which is exactly why the boundary becomes difficult to see from inside.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
The goal, then, is not to expose other people's secret motives. It is to ask whether **my own righteous speech is producing rewards unrelated to helping the victim**. Used against someone else, Kawashima's line can become another weapon:「you are the aggressor now」. Used on oneself, it works better as a brake.

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Grubbs et al., “Moral grandstanding in public discourse,” 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0223749)

## 5. Four questions work better than measuring「who is angrier」

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
If we want to turn the aphorism into a practical check rather than emotional policing, it helps to replace「how angry am I?」with four questions.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
None of these is a perfect test. A victim's preferred outcome is not automatically the best outcome for everyone, and harmed people can minimize what happened to them. Even so, these questions are useful for checking whether a third party has started to drift away from the person supposedly being supported.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
- **Am I listening to what the person wants?** Apology, correction, distance, formal punishment, privacy. Have I ignored their wishes from the start?
- **Is the goal reducing harm?** Is there an exit such as prevention, correction, safety, or repair? Or has making the offender suffer become the goal itself?
- **Am I creating new harm?** Attacking unrelated family members or employers, spreading unverified claims, or condemning the whole person for one act.
- **Can I stop?** If the harmed person says「enough」or「please do not spread this further」, can I actually stop?

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
The last question matters most to me. Support normally has a stop condition because it watches the supported person's state. Punishment can lose that condition. If the demands keep updating—apologize more, lose more, suffer more—then the goal may have moved from repair toward punishment for its own sake.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
These questions turn the too-simple formula「angrier than the victim = aggressor」into something operational: **Can I recenter the victim? Is there an exit toward improvement? Am I avoiding new harm? Can I stop?** The value of the aphorism is not faster condemnation. It is faster self-audit.

## 6. A simple test: if the victim disappeared from the story, would I still want to keep this anger alive?

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Here is an even shorter self-check. While angry, temporarily remove the victim from the story in your head.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Stop thinking about what the person wants, how they are doing now, or what they need restored. Look only at the offender. If your anger becomes more energetic—scrolling for old posts, adding unrelated accusations, searching for fresh material to keep the fire going—that is worth noticing.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
This is a thought experiment for this essay, not a psychological diagnostic method. Research suggests that third-party intervention can take different forms: empathic concern for a victim can relate to helping, while moral outrage is more closely linked to punishment. Other work also shows that compassion for victims can intensify third-party punishment when the suffering is seen as unjust.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Punishment can of course be necessary. The problem is not punishment itself; it is what happens when only punishment remains. The victim recovers, but the anger continues. A system changes, but another target is found. The gaze that began on the harmed person returns to「me, the person who finds and punishes bad people」.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
So I would translate Kawashima's line this way: **when your anger can survive without the victim, check the subject again.** Is it still support? Justice? Or has it become a story that gives you permission to attack?

<!-- level:1 role:source -->
[Lotz, Okimoto & Schlösser, third-party intervention strategies, 2018 summary（CiNii）](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1050575520346697856) ／ [Rudolph et al., “Compassion magnifies third-party punishment,” 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30945902/)

## 7. Use the line to ask whether you can leave the seat of righteousness, not to silence other people

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
At first I liked the sentence simply as a great line. After researching it, I still think it is powerful, but for a slightly different reason.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Third-party anger matters. It can make visible what a victim cannot safely say, enforce norms, and push institutions to change. So「you were not harmed, stay quiet」is not a good rule. Society should not force the entire cost of protest onto the people already harmed.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
But third parties also have freedoms that victims often do not. They can enter when they want, choose stronger language, leave for the next case, and receive the satisfaction of standing on the right side without carrying the original harm. Because that freedom exists, outsiders have a special responsibility to audit their own anger.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
Kawashima's line is not useful because it makes anger smaller. It is useful because it asks us not to misidentify **who owns the anger**. The outrage may have started as an attempt to protect someone. Has it become more interested in my desire to punish, my identity, or my group than in the victim's will and recovery?

Maybe the hardest part is not getting angry.

**It is being able to leave the seat of righteousness when the person you meant to support no longer needs you there.**
