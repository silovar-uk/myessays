# Coopetitionは「仲良くする」話ではない
## It is about designing where competition should happen.

<!-- level:4 role:question -->
Do rivals have to fight everywhere?
<!-- level:2 role:example -->
1997年、経営危機にあったAppleにMicrosoftは150 million dollarsを投資した。
<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
The two firms were fierce rivals in personal computing, but Microsoft also promised to keep supporting Office for Mac, and Apple rebuilt its relationship with Microsoft.
<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
「敵を助けた」と見ると変だが、zoom outしてgame全体を見ると、a rival staying alive can sometimes create value for you too.
<!-- level:5 role:insight -->
The first idea to drop is simple: **a company is not permanently either an enemy or a friend.**

[Harvard Business Review, “When Collaborating with Competitors Makes Sense,” 2024](https://hbr.org/podcast/2024/07/when-collaborating-with-competitors-makes-sense)

---

## 1. Coopetition is not “let’s all get along”

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Coopetition does not replace competition with cooperation.
<!-- level:3 role:context -->
Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff popularized the idea in their 1996 book *Co-opetition*, using game theory to look at situations where firms create value together while still competing over who captures that value.
<!-- level:2 role:example -->
Their later examples include Apple and Samsung, DHL and UPS, Ford and GM, and Google and Yahoo: rivals that can cooperate in one area while competing hard in another.
<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
ここでcooperationは「相手のために譲る」ことではない。It can reduce duplicated cost, share risk, combine different strengths, or make possible something neither side could build alone.
<!-- level:5 role:insight -->
Coopetition changes one assumption more than it changes competitive spirit: **the pie is not always fixed before competition begins.**

[Adam Brandenburger & Barry Nalebuff, “The Rules of Co-opetition,” NYU Stern / Harvard Business Review, 2020–2021](https://www.stern.nyu.edu/experience-stern/faculty-research/rules-co-opetition)

---

## 2. 「あの会社と競う」から “we compete in this activity” へ

<!-- level:4 role:question -->
では、how can two firms cooperate and compete with the same rival at the same time?
<!-- level:3 role:context -->
Maria Bengtsson and Sören Kock studied this question in 2000 and argued that cooperation and competition can be separated by activity inside one relationship.
<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
In the Nordic industries they studied, firms tended to cooperate more in activities farther from the customer and compete more in activities closer to the customer.
<!-- level:2 role:example -->
たとえばR&D、procurement、standards、shared infrastructureでは一緒に動き、sales、brand、customer experienceでは本気で競う、という切り分けが考えられる。
<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Once you see the relationship this way, calling Company A simply a “competitor” or a “partner” becomes too rough.
<!-- level:5 role:insight -->
The unit of strategy shifts from **which company is the enemy** to **which activities should be shared and which should stay different**.

[Maria Bengtsson & Sören Kock, “Coopetition in Business Networks—to Cooperate and Compete Simultaneously,” Industrial Marketing Management, 2000](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S001985019900067X)

## 3. Growing the pie and fighting for the slice are different games

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
A useful way to understand coopetition is to separate value creation from value capture.
<!-- level:2 role:example -->
Two firms may create a common standard so more products work together, or share logistics so each side carries less fixed cost.
<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
At this stage, both sides can gain at the same time.
<!-- level:2 role:contrast -->
But once customers choose which product, brand, or service gets their money, competition returns.
<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
価値創造まで全部competitiveにすると、共有できたcostやknowledgeまで二重化する。But if value capture becomes too cooperative, differentiation and competitive pressure can disappear.
<!-- level:5 role:insight -->
So the real question is not “Should we cooperate?” It is: **what should we grow together, and what should we deliberately keep competitive?**

---

## 4. Good coopetition keeps some tension alive

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
If cooperation creates value, it is tempting to think that more cooperation is always better.
<!-- level:3 role:context -->
But working with a rival creates risks: knowledge leakage, opportunism, unequal power, fights over value capture, and legal limits on coordination between competitors.
<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
A 2026 review in *Review of Managerial Science* analyzed 2,104 articles published from 1996 to 2024 and grouped the field into three themes: designing, managing, and sustaining coopetition.
<!-- level:2 role:evidence -->
The review argues that trust and tension are not simple opposites; they can grow and change together, and sustainable coopetition requires the ability to keep recalibrating the balance between cooperation and competition.
<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
成功とは、tensionがゼロになることではない。
<!-- level:5 role:insight -->
**Strong coopetition cooperates enough not to break, competes enough not to become dull, and keeps the boundary movable.**

[Aziz Kemal Konyalıoğlu, Aylin Ates & Steve Paton, “Designing, managing and sustaining coopetition,” Review of Managerial Science, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11846-026-00994-2)

## 5. Sports leagues make the idea easier to see

<!-- level:4 role:question -->
Move the idea from companies to a sports league, and cooperating while competing stops looking strange.
<!-- level:2 role:example -->
Clubs compete in matches, player recruitment, brand, tickets, sponsors, and local support.
<!-- level:2 role:example -->
一方で、league awareness、the number of people watching the sport、common rules、broadcasting or data infrastructure、safety、the overall viewing cultureなどは、一クラブだけで育てるより共同で大きくした方がvalueが増える領域になりうる。
<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
If every rival’s growth is treated as your loss, you quietly assume the whole market is a fixed pie.
<!-- level:4 role:development -->
But after the market grows, clubs can still compete fiercely over who earns more attention, loyalty, and revenue.
<!-- level:5 role:insight -->
Sometimes **a stronger rival helps create a stronger arena in which you can compete.**

---

## 6. Strategy may be less about “who do we beat?” and more about “where do we compete?”

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
The phrase competitive strategy naturally makes us ask how to beat a rival.
<!-- level:2 role:recap -->
But coopetition research shows that the same firms can compete and cooperate at once, and the boundary can change by activity, customer proximity, resources, time, and governance.
<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
From this view, competition is not a permanent relationship between two companies. It is closer to a mechanism we place where it creates useful pressure.
<!-- level:4 role:development -->
If firms fight over shared foundations, the market may stay small; if they share everything near the customer, they may lose the reason to be chosen.
<!-- level:5 role:conclusion -->
The deeper strategic question is therefore not “competition or cooperation?” It is: **what do we build together, and from what point onward do we compete for real?**

---

## 5 questions for practical use

When you look at a rival, try these questions in order.

1. **What pie could we grow together?** 市場規模、認知、standards、infrastructure、demandのうち、共同投資の方が効くものは何か。
2. **What can we share far from the customer?** R&D、procurement、base technology、data formats、educationなど、共有しても独自価値を壊しにくい活動は何か。
3. **Where must competition remain?** Brand、customer experience、product design、salesなど、自社が選ばれる理由を作る活動は何か。
4. **What is our secret sauce?** 共有で得るbenefitよりknowledge leakageやdependencyのriskが大きくなる境界はどこか。
5. **When should the boundary move?** Market conditions、power balance、technologyが変わったとき、cooperation and competitionをどうrecalibrateするか。

These questions reveal a large strategic space between “they are competitors, so no” and “let’s cooperate for the industry.”
Coopetition is not a compromise halfway between two pure strategies.
**It is a design skill: put cooperation and competition in different places for different purposes.**
