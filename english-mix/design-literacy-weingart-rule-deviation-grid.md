# You Can’t Break the Grid Until You Can See It――Wolfgang Weingart
## Design Literacy #26｜ルールを壊すのではなく、design the difference from a visible baseline

「もう少し自由にしてください」

That sounds simple, but it is a surprisingly difficult design request.

What does “free” actually mean?

文字を斜めにする？サイズをばらす？色を増やす？alignmentを外す？

If you do all of them, yes, you reduce the number of rules.

でも、**fewer rules does not automatically mean more expression.**

今回の問いはこれだ。

**What is the difference between breaking a rule and having no rule at all?**

---

## 1. まず一言：Deviation needs somewhere to return to

**Breaking a rule is different from having no rule.**

When one element leaves a grid, the deviation becomes visible because the surrounding layout still says, “this is where things normally align.”

たとえば6-column gridの中で、ほとんどの要素が同じreference lineを使っている。

```text
| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |

TITLE      |---|---|---|
TEXT       |---|---|---|
DATE       |---|---|
IMAGE              |---|---|
CTA          |---|---|
```

Only the CTA shifts slightly.

Then the shift has tension.

では、everything leaves the rule at onceならどうなるか。

```text
   TITLE
             TEXT
 DATE
        IMAGE
                 CTA
```

Now it becomes hard to tell what the “deviation” is.

The baseline has disappeared.

**Deviation needs a visible baseline.**

That is the central claim of this lesson.

---

## 2. Was Wolfgang Weingart simply “the guy who rejected Swiss Typography”?

Wolfgang Weingart（1941–2021）はBaselでtypographyを教え、後にNew Wave Typographyと強く結び付けて語られるdesigner / educatorだ。

His work includes expanded letter spacing, diagonal movement, collisions of scale and weight, overlays, and layouts that push against the regularity of the grid.

Cooper Hewitt describes Weingart’s late-1960s Basel work as challenging traditional modern graphic-design rules and subverting the rigid regularity of the grid, with later influence on New Wave graphics.

でも、ここを

```text
Swiss Style
↓
reject everything
↓
freedom
```

と理解すると粗すぎる。

Museum für Gestaltung Zürich positions him as a **“tradition-conscious innovator.”**

Weingart himself later explained that Swiss Typography contained qualities he did not want to throw away completely. He used those qualities as a base from which to search for new typographic territory.

A better model is:

```text
Swiss Typography
↓
learn the fundamentals
↓
observe what the constraints do
↓
experiment with bending them
↓
search for new expressive possibilities
```

つまり、rejectionより**experimentation from inside the system**に近い。

---

## 3. “Know the rules before breaking them” was not just a moral lesson

Weingart famously wrote that the only way to break typographic rules was to know them.

That can sound like a generic master-disciple slogan.

But his practice was much more concrete.

Metal typesetting gave him physical access to variables such as:

- letter spacing
- slant
- weight
- size
- type character
- limits of legibility

He could manipulate one relationship and see what changed.

後年のinterviewでも、Baselでは以前ほどstrictにrulesへ従わない一方で、some elementary rules are still necessary, and meaningless chaos is not the goalと話している。

So “break the rules” does not mean:

**Rule OFF**

It is closer to:

**Rule → Variable → Experiment**

You know what you are changing, so the change becomes an experiment.

---

## 4. Break one variable first

A useful production exercise is to create three versions of the same event graphic.

### A｜Keep everything stable

```text
URAWA REDS
9.19 SAT
VS TOKYO V
SPECIAL EVENT
```

- left aligned
- same direction
- one typeface
- regular spacing

Stable and predictable.

### B｜Break one variable

```text
URAWA REDS
9.19 SAT
        VS TOKYO V
SPECIAL EVENT
```

Only alignment changes.

The other rules stay visible.

Now the shift of “VS TOKYO V” becomes legible as a decision.

### C｜Break everything

```text
     URAWA
 REDS
        9.19
   / SAT
VS
      TOKYO V
 SPECIAL
       EVENT
```

Position, angle, size and spacing all move at once.

This may create a strong result.

でも、**which variable created which effect becomes harder to diagnose.**

So the practical rule is:

**Keep one stable rule and break one variable first.**

最初の試作では、一変数だけ外して、その差にmeaningがあるかを見る。

---

## 5. 制作・修正指示へ翻訳する

“もっと遊んでください”や“break the grid”だけでは、何を変更するのかが曖昧すぎる。

Use this instead:

> **全体の基準となるgrid、typeface、information hierarchyのうち最低一つは維持してください。そのうえで、主役となる一要素だけ、position・angle・size・letter spacingのいずれか一変数を意図的に外してください。First test one deviation. Add a second only if the first has a clear job.**

A shorter review question is:

> **What rule are you breaking, and can we still see that rule after you break it?**

答えられないなら、controlled deviationではなくrandom variationかもしれない。

---

## 6. MoMA’s titles reveal an interesting sequence

MoMA holds Weingart’s *Typographic Process* works from roughly 1971–74.

The series includes titles such as:

- *Organized Text Structures*
- *From Simple to Complex*
- *Typographic Signs*
- *Typography as (Painting)*

特に面白いのは**From Simple to Complex**。

We cannot reconstruct an entire teaching philosophy from a title alone.

However, when these works are read alongside Weingart’s writing and interviews, a pattern appears: start from basic design relationships, then expand their possibilities step by step.

**FACT:** MoMA documents these works, their titles and dates.

**INTERPRETATION:** For a designer today, copying the final “New Wave look” is less useful than reproducing the experimental process: begin with a simple system, then manipulate one variable at a time.

---

## 7. The dangerous misunderstanding: “Make it look Weingart”

Here is the irony.

Weingart later criticized the way his results were copied as a visual “Weingart style.”

He argued that people had taken the vocabulary without understanding the method, material and underlying structure that produced it.

So this equation is weak:

```text
diagonal type
+
wide letter spacing
+
overlap
+
broken grid
=
Weingart
```

The same problem appears today with requests such as:

- “Bauhausっぽく”
- “Swissっぽく”
- “Brutalismっぽく”
- “New Waveっぽく”

When history becomes a preset, we copy visible symptoms and lose the logic behind them.

Weingart is more useful if we ask:

**What was the baseline? What did he question? Which variable did he move?**

---

## 8. 守破離との接続：similar structure, not historical lineage

This process looks surprisingly close to the Japanese learning model of **守破離**.

```text
守
internalize the rule

破
treat the rule as an experimental object

離
build your own judgment instead of copying a style
```

But there is no historical claim here that Weingart studied or referenced守破離.

This is a **USEFUL COMPARISON**, not documented influence.

The comparison helps us redefine freedom.

**Freedom is not escaping rules. It is being able to choose your distance from them.**

---

## 9. 以前の学びとの接続

In Design Literacy #11, Grid was defined as a **shared coordinate system for repeatable decisions**.

That model still holds.

Today we add another function.

```text
Grid
creates shared coordinates
↓
Expectation
creates “where this should align”
↓
Deviation
makes difference visible
↓
Emphasis / Tension
turns difference into meaning
```

So a grid is not only a tool for alignment.

**It is also the baseline that lets misalignment become readable.**

Visual Hierarchy also returns here.

A difference is not enough. The difference needs a job.

---

## 10. 30-second observation

Open one poster or interface.

First find the most repeated alignment.

Then find the element that moves furthest away from it.

Ask:

> **What became stronger because this element broke the rule?**

If you can answer, the deviation probably has a job.

If the only answer is “it feels more dynamic,” go back and inspect the baseline again.

---

## 11. Next concept: Constraint

This lesson framed rules as something we can keep or bend.

The next layer is **Constraint**.

- Grid
- spacing scale
- type scale
- brand rule
- component
- device width
- accessibility requirement

All of them reduce possible choices.

So the next question is:

**Do constraints block creativity, or can they make meaningful differences easier to discover?**

---

## FACT / INTERPRETATION

**FACT**

- Museum für Gestaltung Zürich describes Weingart as a tradition-conscious innovator and studies his work through the relationship between tradition, media change and innovation.
- MoMA holds the *Typographic Process* works from 1971–74.
- Cooper Hewitt connects Weingart’s challenges to grid regularity and letter spacing with the development of New Wave graphics.
- Weingart’s own writing and interviews show both a desire to push against Swiss Typography and a continued belief in elementary rules and disciplined judgment.
- He later criticized superficial imitation of a “Weingart style.”

**INTERPRETATION**

- For contemporary UI / graphic design, Weingart is more reusable as a method of controlled deviation than as a visual style preset.
- 守破離 is a comparison for understanding the learning structure, not a claim of historical influence.
