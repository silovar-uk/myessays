# “Clean the Desk” Is Not a Goal
## まずDoneを定義する

A to-do list says:

**片づけをする。**

Nothing strange there.

But imagine moving everything from the desk into one box.

Task completed.

The desk looks clean.

翌朝、必要な資料が見つからない。

Did we succeed?

The strange thing is that “clean the desk” contains no success condition.

How clean?

何が残る？

How fast should important material be retrievable?

Does the system need to survive tomorrow?

Only the verb is defined.

That makes me wonder whether work should begin not with **what to do**, but with **what must be true when the work is done**.

And is that the same thing as defining the “issue”?

Not quite.

---

## 1. Specific goals often beat “do your best” / 何を達成するかを決める

Locke and Latham’s 2002 review summarized decades of Goal Setting Theory research.

One central finding is that, under appropriate conditions, **specific and difficult goals tend to produce higher performance than vague instructions such as “do your best.”**

Goals focus attention.

努力量を変える。

They influence persistence.

必要なstrategyを探すきっかけにもなる。

Compare:

“Clean the desk.”

and

“By 18:00, leave only the laptop, notebook and drink on the desk, and make every daily-use document retrievable within 30 seconds.”

The second statement helps judge actions.

全部を箱に隠す方法では30秒以内に出せない。

So that action fails the goal even if the surface looks clean.

A goal is not only motivational language.

**It is a rule for selecting actions.**

[Locke & Latham, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12237980/)

---

## 2. But outcome goals are not always best / 達成状態を決めれば全部解決、でもない

Here comes the inconvenient part.

When a task is complex and unfamiliar, a hard performance goal can sometimes hurt learning.

1996年の研究では、simple scheduling taskではoutcome goalが有効だった一方、complex taskでは**learning goal**を与えられたparticipantsのperformanceが高かった。

A 2001 study on a moderately complex task found a similar pattern: participants who needed to acquire new task knowledge benefited from specific challenging learning goals.

Instead of:

“Raise sales by 20%.”

A learning goal might be:

“Discover and test four effective strategies for improving conversion.”

結果ではなく、方法の獲得を達成対象にする。

So “goal setting is everything” is too simple.

The type of goal must match the uncertainty of the task.

やり方が分かるならoutcomeを具体化する。

If the method is unknown, make learning itself the target first.

[Winters & Latham, 1996](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1059601196212007)

[Seijts et al., 2001](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/job.70)

[Seijts & Latham, 2005](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/AME.2005.15841964)

## 3. Is the desired outcome the “issue”? / 目標とイシューは近いけど別

In consulting-style problem solving, an **issue tree** starts from a core question and breaks it into smaller answerable questions.

So an issue is closer to **what must be answered or resolved** than to the outcome itself.

For example:

**Goal / 目標**
Increase attendance by 10% year over year.

**Issue / イシュー**
Which barrier matters most: awareness, purchase conversion, scheduling, or lack of companions?

**Task / タスク**
Analyze survey data and purchase behavior to test those hypotheses.

A useful shorthand is:

**Goal = where to go.**

**Issue = what to resolve.**

**Task = what to do.**

They are connected, but collapsing them into one thing makes work blurry.

[Issue Tree — MECE Glossary](https://www.mece.in/glossary/issue-tree)

[ロジックツリー・イシューツリー（論点ツリー）](https://www.concord-career.com/dictionary/logic-tree/)

## 4. Rewrite “clean the desk” three ways / 片づけを分解する

### Task / タスク

**片づけをする。**

An action happened. No result is guaranteed.

### Goal / 目標

**By 18:00, leave only the laptop, notebook and drink on the desk, with every daily-use document retrievable within 30 seconds.**

終了条件がある。

### Issue / イシュー

**Why does the desk return to chaos within three days?**

Then break it down.

- no fixed home for items?
- 収納場所と使用頻度が合っていない？
- no temporary landing zone?
- 捨てる判断基準がない？
- too much stuff for the available space?

Now the task changes.

The solution may be assigning locations, changing storage, creating a temporary tray, or reducing volume.

「片づける」と先に決めると、diagnosisを飛ばす。

A verb can quietly close a question before we have solved it.

---

## 5. Be suspicious of work that begins with a verb / 動詞で始まる仕事を疑う

### “Strengthen social media” / SNSを強化する

What would be different when it is “stronger”?

Maybe:

“In three months, increase average non-follower reach by 30% while maintaining profile-visit conversion.”

Then the issue becomes:

Is the bottleneck exposure, content response, or conversion?

### “Improve meetings” / 会議を改善する

Maybe the outcome is:

“At the end of every 30-minute meeting, each issue has a recorded decision, owner and deadline.”

The issue might be:

Is the delay caused by too many topics, absent decision-makers, or missing pre-read information?

### “Study English” / 英語を勉強する

Maybe the outcome is:

“In three months, explain my work for five minutes without a script and answer five expected questions.”

If the skill is still unfamiliar, a learning goal may be better:

“Identify 10 conversation patterns where I get stuck and acquire three usable chunks for each.”

Words like “do,” “improve,” “strengthen,” and “consider” feel productive.

でも、**they can hide the definition of success.**

---

## 6. Write the end screen before the to-do list / 終了画面を先に書く

A simple four-line template:

### 1. Outcome
What is true when this is finished?

### 2. Evidence
How will we know it is true?

### 3. Issue
What important question is still unanswered?

### 4. Task
What do we do next to answer it or create the desired state?

For a promotion project:

**Outcome**: One campaign direction is agreed, and all relevant teams can explain the same priority audience.

**Evidence**: Audience, proposition, channels and KPI fit on one approved page.

**Issue**: Which attendance barrier is most movable right now—awareness, price, scheduling, or companion availability?

**Task**: Compare existing surveys, purchase data and past campaigns, then narrow the barrier hypotheses to three.

The task comes last.

---

## 7. The wording of the problem shapes the solution / 問題の言い方が解き方を決める

Problem-framing research and practice make a related point: people often jump into solution mode before checking whether they are solving the right problem.

「片づける」と書けば、solutionは片づけに固定される。

Write “run ads,” and the possibility of not advertising disappears.

「イベントをやる」と書けば、イベントが必要かという問いが消える。

Verbs make work feel active.

They also close possibility space.

That may be why complex work should sometimes begin without a verb.

[Harvard Business Review, The Secret to Better Problem Solving, 2016](https://hbr.org/podcast/2016/12/the-secret-to-better-problem-solving)

[Harvard Business Review, Struggling to Solve a Problem? Try Reframing It, 2020](https://hbr.org/2020/11/struggling-to-solve-a-problem-try-reframing-it)

## 8. Define Done before ToDo / 「何をするか」より「何を終わらせるか」

I started with the thought:

「達成すべきことの設定がすべてかもしれない。」

Now I would weaken “everything.”

A specific outcome can be useful, but for complex unfamiliar work, learning goals may be better than performance goals.

So first ask what kind of work this is.

成果を出すphaseか。

Or a strategy-discovery phase?

Then separate three things.

Goal: the finished state.

Issue: the important unanswered question.

Task: the action taken to answer it or build the state.

That is why “clean the desk” was not enough.

It described motion, not completion.

仕事の最初に書くべきなのは、ToDoではないのかもしれない。

**Define Done first.**

Once the end state is clear, unnecessary work becomes visible too.
