# 「ン」で置くより、hold the sound――英語は「伸ばし」のつもりで発音すると何が変わるか
## Longer is not the point. Nasals, syllabic consonants, and Japanese moraic N reveal what “hold the gesture” can actually mean

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
英語を話していると、word-final consonantsが妙に「点」になることがある。たとえば`man`を「マン」と言うとき、we produce the vowel and then place a small 「ン」 at the end. ところが最後の`n`を一個置くのでなく、舌先を上の歯茎付近につけたまま`nnnn`と**hold the sound state for a moment**と考えると、急にEnglish contourへ近づいたように感じることがある。

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
At first, the hypothesis is rough: “Maybe English should feel less like adding a moraic nasal and more like sustaining a sound.” しかし調べ始めると、one part makes strong phonetic sense and another part collapses. English really does contain consonants whose articulatory state can be sustained. 一方で、**Japanese word-final nasals have been reported as relatively longer than English ones**. So “make it longer and it becomes English” points in the wrong direction.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
The useful version is not about raw duration. It is about **not appending a separate kana-like beat, but entering a consonant gesture, sustaining it as needed, and carrying it into what comes next**. 本稿では、この意味での「伸ばし」を“hold the gesture”として扱う。

![「ン」を別の一拍として置くイメージと、英語の子音の調音状態を保つイメージを比較した模式図。](https://silovar-uk.github.io/myessays/assets/english-final-consonants-hold-gesture-timeline.svg)

*図1：「ンを足す」から“hold the consonant”へ。This is a pedagogical timing diagram, not a measured duration graph.*

## 1. まず用語を直す。「撥音便」ではなく、ここで問題なのは撥音「ん」である

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
One terminology correction first. 「撥音便」は国語学では、`読みて→読んで`や`死にて→死んで`のようにa form changes into a nasalized form. 今回比べたいのはそのhistorical/grammatical processではなく、現代日本語の**撥音「ん」**を一単位として感じることと、English consonantsをどう感じるかである。文化庁も現代仮名遣いで「ん」を撥音として整理している。

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
In phonology, Japanese 「ん」 is often represented as the moraic nasal `/N/`. A mora is a key timing/rhythmic unit in Japanese, and the final 「ん」 in something like `か・ん` can occupy one mora. 前川喜久雄（Kikuo Maekawa）のreal-time MRI studyでも、日本語`/N/`はindependent mora statusを持つnasal segmentとして扱われる。

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
This can become a learning trap. 「ン」は文字としてもrhythmic unitとしても、a discrete piece with its own slotに見えやすい。するとEnglish `n`にも“vowel + one N piece”という処理を持ち込みやすい。But English `n` is not a substitute kana. It is an **articulatory gesture** with tongue position, velum setting, voicing, and temporal overlap with neighboring sounds.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
So the first value of the “hold it” metaphor is not greater duration. It is that it reminds us that **consonants also occupy time and have a bodily posture**, even when the spelling shows only one letter.

**主な根拠**：[文化庁「現代仮名遣い」](https://www.bunka.go.jp/kokugo_nihongo/sisaku/joho/joho/kijun/naikaku/gendaikana/honbun_dai1.html) ／ [「撥音便」の定義](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E6%92%A5%E9%9F%B3%E4%BE%BF-602231) ／ [Maekawa, “Production of the utterance-final moraic nasal in Japanese”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0025100321000050)

## 2. 英語の`n`は、瞬間ではなく「保てる状態」である

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
There is a physical reason why English `n` can feel different when you imagine sustaining it. `/n/` is a voiced alveolar nasal: the tongue makes an oral closure around the alveolar region while the velum lowers and lets air escape through the nose. 口では閉じているのに鼻からはairflowが続き、通常はvoicingも続く。だから`nnnn`と**the same configuration can be maintained**.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
The same basic logic applies to `/m/` and `/ŋ/`. With `/m/`, the lips close while nasal airflow continues; with `/ŋ/`, the back of the tongue contacts the velar area. English has the major nasal phonemes `/m n ŋ/`, distinguished by place. `sum /sʌm/`, `sun /sʌn/`, and `sung /sʌŋ/` form a neat near-minimal set where changing the nasal changes the word.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Kana-like renderings make all three endings look like “some nasal piece at the end.” But English asks a more specific question: **where are you holding the closure while the nasal sound continues?** `m` lives at the lips, `n` at the alveolar region, `ŋ` farther back. The “stretch” cue is useful not because it exaggerates duration, but because it helps you keep the target place instead of abandoning it instantly.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
If you think of final consonants only as “pronounced or not pronounced,” they become points. If you think in terms of **what posture stays active for how long**, they become lines. Part of English timing lives in that line.

![有声歯茎鼻音[n]の発音時の口腔断面模式図。](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Voiced_alveolar_nasal.svg)

*図2：Voiced alveolar nasal [n]. The oral passage is closed at the alveolar region while air can flow through the nasal cavity. 画像：Nardog / Tavin, Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/)。*

**主な根拠**：[Essentials of Linguistics「子音の分類」](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/essentialsoflinguistics/chapter/2-7-classifying-consonants/) ／ [An Introduction to American English Phonetics「Nasals」](https://opentextbooks.rug.nl/americanenglishphonetics2/chapter/12-1-nasals/)

## 3. ところが「日本語より長くする」は、証拠と逆向きになりうる

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Here the hypothesis breaks in a useful way. If “stretch” literally means **make English nasals longer than Japanese nasals**, the evidence can point the other way.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
Naoya Mori’s 2023 recording study comparing Japanese and English word-final nasals reported a ratio of word-initial to word-final nasal duration of `1:1.53` for English and `1:2.33` for Japanese. これはlimited experimental settingの一研究で、every speaker, word, or speech rateへ一般化できない。Still, it directly warns against the easy story “Japanese N is short, English n is long.”

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
Yumiko Sato’s 1993 study compared syllable-final nasal durations across Japanese, English, and Korean and discussed the distinctive moraic status of Japanese final nasals. English nasal duration also varies with position, neighboring segments, stress, and rate. 長さはlanguage label一個で決まる固定値ではない。

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
This counterevidence lets us rescue the metaphor more precisely. The target is **not more milliseconds**. The target is to stop treating `n` as “a Japanese N appended after the vowel,” and instead move into the `n` articulation and keep it for the amount of time the context requires. It can be short in real time and still be continuous as a gesture.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
A teaching metaphor does not have to be literally true in every acoustic measurement. But it must be clear about **what variable it is trying to change**. Here, “stretch” should change attentional organization, not prescribe longer segment duration.

> **この稿での「伸ばす」**
>
> Not “make it longer.”  
> Hold the consonant’s articulatory place and airflow/voicing state for the necessary interval.  
> Move away from the feeling of adding one separate Japanese 「ン」 mora.

**主な根拠**：[森直哉「日本語と英語における語末鼻子音の長さの比較」](https://doi.org/10.57375/0002000027) ／ [Sato, “The Durations of Syllable-Final Nasals and the Mora Hypothesis in Japanese”](https://doi.org/10.1159/000261925)

## 4. 日本語の「ん」は一種類に見えるが、実際の舌はかなり変身している

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Japanese 「ん」 itself is already stranger than the spelling suggests. The kana is one symbol, but the articulatory location changes substantially with context.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
Maekawa’s real-time MRI work found that Japanese moraic `/N/` tends to share the place of articulation of a following consonant when one exists. At utterance-final position, too, the old simple story “it is always uvular” did not hold: closure location ranged widely and was strongly influenced by the preceding vowel.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
So the 「ん」 in `さんぽ` shifts toward the lips, while the 「ん」 before a velar consonant shifts farther back. Native speakers normally do not consciously split these into different phonemes; they hear and write one `ん`. 音声としては変身するのに、phonologically it is packaged as one unit.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
English packages the system differently. `/m/`, `/n/`, and `/ŋ/` can distinguish words in coda position. Of course connected English also shows assimilation, so real speech is not a sequence of frozen dictionary targets. それでもlearning targetとしては、“all of them are N”より**lip m, alveolar n, back-of-tongue ŋ**を分けるほうが、contrastを残せる。

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
What Japanese learners may lose is not nasality itself but the **place information hidden by the psychologically convenient single box called 「ン」**. The “hold” cue reopens that box and gives each English nasal a place to live.

**主な根拠**：[Maekawa (2021/2023)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0025100321000050) ／ [Otakeほか「The representation of Japanese moraic nasals」](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.417239) ／ [Peperkampほか「On the perceptual origin of loanword adaptations」](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952675708001425)

## 5. `button`や`cotton`では、子音が「音節の中心」になることさえある

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
An even more extreme English pattern shows that “giving consonants time” is not merely a motivational trick. English can let a **consonant itself serve as the syllabic nucleus**.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
These are syllabic consonants. In words such as `cotton`, `button`, `listen`, and `sudden`, depending on accent and speaking style, the weak vowel can be highly reduced and `n` may be analyzed as syllabic `[n̩]`. An open phonetics textbook explicitly teaches learners to start with `[ən]`, remove the vowel, and then “linger” on the syllabic `[n̩]`.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
That makes the physical lesson unusually clear. Instead of creating a “to + N” sequence with a separate beat, the speaker can move into the `n` closure and let **the nasal itself carry the syllable**. もちろんall speakers do not produce all such words identically, and schwa-containing variants exist.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Still, the structural implication matters. A consonant is not merely a tiny separator between vowels. Under the right conditions it has enough temporal and acoustic substance to shoulder a syllable. **Giving the consonant somewhere to stay** is not foreign to English phonology.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
So “stretch” gains a better meaning here: not adding more sound, but allowing a consonant to stand on its own when the vowel weakens. 発音を足す比喩ではなく、余計な母音を引くための比喩になる。

**主な根拠**：[Introducing the IPA「Syllables」](https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/introducingtheipa/chapter/syllables/) ／ [UC Berkeley “Examples of the Consonant [syllabic]”](https://linguistics.berkeley.edu/~kjohnson/English_Phonetics/cons_syllabic.html)

## 6. この方法は鼻音だけではない。ただし、全部の英語音へ広げると壊れる

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Can we now generalize: “Just stretch all English sounds”? No. Sounds differ in whether they behave like **sustainable states or brief events**.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Nasals `/m n ŋ/` can sustain nasal airflow and usually voicing. Fricatives `/f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ/` can sustain turbulence through a narrow constriction: `ffff`, `ssss`, `zzzz`. Approximants such as `/l ɹ/` can maintain a posture to some extent. `/w j/` are glides, however, so the movement toward the next vowel is part of their identity.

<!-- level:2 role:qualification -->
Stops `/p t k b d g/` are different. They require complete oral closure followed by release. You can hold the closure, but you cannot sustain the burst itself as a steady `tttt` sound. Affricates `/tʃ dʒ/` combine stop closure with frication, so “stretch it” again means something more complex.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
This gives us a practical “hold test.” Produce the sound and ask whether you can keep that acoustic/articulatory state for about half a second. `nnnn`, `ssss`, and `mmmm` work; `tttt` does not. それだけで、sounds that can be practiced as sustained states and sounds that need closure-release timing start to separate.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
The principle is therefore not “English is stretched.” It is: **for sounds that can be sustained, practice not dropping the state too early; for sounds that cannot, practice the timing of closure and release.** One metaphor becomes useful only after it is split by phonetic mechanism.

## 7. 「伸ばす」の本当の効き目は、語末で止めず、次の音まで橋を残すことにある

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Another problem with treating a word-final consonant as a point is that the mouth “finishes the word” too completely. Real English happens in connected speech, where words do not appear one by one like dictionary entries.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Take `turn it on`. If `turn` ends as a completed Japanese-style “ターン。” and only then `it` begins, the motor sequence restarts at the boundary. If instead you form the `n` and keep the tongue position alive as you move into the next vowel, the cross-word connection becomes easier to feel. In connected speech, segment durations and articulations differ from isolated words, and processes such as assimilation, reduction, and linking emerge.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Here “hold” does not mean making the consonant conspicuously long. It means **do not throw away the current gesture before the next gesture is ready**. The ending of one sound becomes part of the beginning of the next.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
If fluency is defined as “speak faster,” learners may rush to delete things. But before deletion comes continuity. **Speed can be an outcome of uninterrupted coordination rather than its cause.**

**主な根拠**：[Cambridge Dictionary “connected speech”](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/connected-speech) ／ [Peperkampほか（2008）](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952675708001425)

## 8. 5分でできる「保つ」実験は、長さではなく身体の場所を観察する

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
You can test the idea with nothing more than your mouth and a recorder. Say the same word in two versions. The goal is not to vote on “which sounds more native,” but to observe **where your articulation disappears too early**.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
First: `man`. Compare “ma + one N beat” with a version where you move from the vowel into alveolar `n`, keep the tongue closure, and let nasal voicing continue briefly before ending. Second: `team`. Compare ending the instant the lips close with keeping the closed-lip `m` and nasal airflow alive.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Third: `sing`. Find the back-of-tongue closure for `ŋ` and sustain the nasal there. Fourth: compare `miss` and `sit`. The final `s` in `miss` can sustain frication; the `t` in `sit` cannot be sustained in the same way. この差が分かれば、“hold” is no longer a vague English mindset but a tool for distinguishing articulatory mechanisms.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
When you listen back, do not judge only length. Check: ①did an extra vowel appear at the end, ②did the nasal/fricative place collapse, ③if another word follows, did you completely stop before it? Slow playback if useful. **Locate the motor failure before rating the accent.**

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
This is more reproducible than trying to copy an entire native recording at once. Keep what can be kept, move when movement is required, and use tongue, lips, and nasal airflow as sensors. **Your mouth becomes part of the measuring equipment.**

## 9. 実務的な練習原則は「長く」ではなく「切らずに、足さずに、場所を残す」

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
For practice, “make it longer” is too crude. A better cue is: **don’t cut it early, don’t add an extra vowel, and keep the articulatory place as long as the context needs**.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Start with nasals. Contrast `sum / sun / sung`: lips for `m`, alveolar contact for `n`, back-of-tongue contact for `ŋ`. Then use final-nasal words such as `team / ten / sing` and check that you are not adding `mu / nu / gu`-like vowels. After that, move to weak syllables in `button` or `listen`, where the task may be to reduce an unnecessary vowel and let the consonant carry more weight.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Next, expand to fricatives. Feel the final `f` in `safe`, `s` in `miss`, and `z` in `is` as intervals of airflow rather than terminal letters. Then move to short phrases and let the word-final consonant transition into the next vowel without resetting the whole mouth.

<!-- level:3 role:qualification -->
Do not exaggerate every consonant. English segment duration is context-sensitive, and over-lengthening can sound unnatural. Before copying exact duration, verify the more basic constraints: **the sound did not vanish, no extra vowel appeared, and the articulatory place moved cleanly into what followed**.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
The aim is not to erase katakana from your brain. It is to recover the **continuous timing and motion that katakana’s convenient boxes can hide**. Turn a consonant back from a letter into a short-lived bodily action.

## 10. 調べる前より、「伸ばす」は短い言葉になった

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
The original idea was: “Maybe English works better if I pronounce it as a stretch rather than like a Japanese nasal beat.” After research, that sentence is too broad. But it is still worth keeping in a smaller form.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
“撥音便” was not the right technical term. We could not claim English nasals are simply longer. Stops cannot be sustained in the same sense. Yet nasals and fricatives do support sustained articulatory states, English can even make consonants syllabic, and the single Japanese category 「ん」 can psychologically collapse place distinctions that English keeps separate.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
What survives is smaller and more useful: **not “stretch the English consonant,” but “don’t throw away the consonant before the next gesture is ready.”** Final `n` is not a dot placed after the vowel. You enter it, hold its configuration for the needed interval, and hand that posture to the next sound. English starts to look a little less like reading letters and a little more like passing postures from one moment to the next.

> **一文で残すなら**
>
> Don’t make the consonant longer for its own sake. Don’t abandon its posture too early.

## 11. 再リサーチ用プロンプト――「伸ばし」を感覚論で終わらせない

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
This topic can balloon quickly if pronunciation metaphors and acoustic measurements are mixed together. Re-research must separate **duration, place of articulation, syllable structure, connected speech, and L2 learning**.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
The prompt below is designed to falsify this essay, not protect it. In particular, it forces a search for counterevidence before accepting intuitive claims such as “English is longer” or “Japanese is shorter.”

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
A metaphor becomes useful not because it escapes measurement, but because we can identify **exactly where it works and where it stops working**.

```text
You are a researcher working across phonetics, phonology, second-language pronunciation, and Japanese linguistics.
Research the hypothesis: “For Japanese learners of English, it may be more useful to imagine certain English consonants as sounds/gestures to be sustained rather than as a Japanese moraic ‘ン’ appended after a vowel.”

1. First distinguish Japanese 撥音便, 撥音 /N/, mora, English nasal phonemes /m n ŋ/, syllabic consonants, coda consonants, and connected speech.
2. Treat “sustain/hold” as an articulatory teaching metaphor, not as a claim that English consonants are universally longer.
3. Search primary acoustic/articulatory studies comparing Japanese and English nasal duration. Record participants, word position, phonetic context, speaking rate, and measurement method.
4. Search articulatory descriptions of English /m n ŋ/ and Japanese moraic /N/. Separate phonemic representation from phonetic allophony and assimilation.
5. Search English syllabic /n l m/ and identify dialect/style variation. Do not imply every speaker always deletes schwa.
6. Classify English consonants by whether a steady articulatory/acoustic state can be sustained: nasals, fricatives, approximants, stops, affricates. Explain why “hold” means different things for each.
7. Investigate whether “holding the gesture until the next sound” is compatible with connected-speech research on coarticulation, linking, assimilation, and reduction.
8. Search specifically for Japanese-L1 English production studies. Do not infer learner errors from Japanese phonology alone.
9. Label every major statement FACT, AUTHOR INTERPRETATION, or PEDAGOGICAL PROPOSAL.
10. Actively search for counterevidence to “English final nasals are longer than Japanese final nasals.”
11. Produce minimal or near-minimal English examples that isolate nasal place, such as sum/sun/sung, and verify their IPA in a reputable dictionary.
12. End with a practice protocol that changes articulatory attention without prescribing exaggerated duration.
13. Attach a DOI, publisher, university, or society source link to every major empirical claim.
```
