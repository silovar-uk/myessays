# The shark is a lemon――名前が二つあるレモンザメと、見えない海の一生

*Research date: 2026-10-25. Sources are linked throughout; this is not original fieldwork.*

A shark named after a lemon sounds almost friendly. But the first surprise is not its color. **日本語の「レモンザメ」と英語の“lemon shark”は、通常は別種を指す。** Even a seemingly simple animal name can lead us to the wrong research.

For example, the famous experiment showing that lemon sharks can learn by watching others is real. **ただし被験個体はニシレモンザメだった。** So we start with naming, then examine body structure, nurseries, reproductive ecology, research methods and conservation. Facts and interpretations will stay separate.

## 守｜A lemon-colored shark is not a species ID

Sharks are fish, not marine mammals. They breathe with gills and have largely cartilaginous skeletons. 日本のレモンザメはメジロザメ科の**Negaprion acutidens**。Its English name is **sicklefin lemon shark**: “sicklefin” refers to the curved fins. Confirmed with the [Okinawa Churaumi Aquarium](https://churaumi.okinawa/fishbook/00000496/) and the [World Register of Marine Species](https://www.marinespecies.org/aphia.php?id=217356&p=taxdetails).

By contrast, the widely studied **lemon shark, Negaprion brevirostris**, is ニシレモンザメ in Japanese. The two species are related and look alike, but their ranges differ: Indo-Pacific versus Atlantic and the American side of the Pacific. **Similar names are not interchangeable scientific evidence.** [Florida Museum](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/discover-fish/species-profiles/sicklefin-lemon-shark/) · [IUCN assessment for N. brevirostris](https://doi.org/10.2305/IUCN.UK.2021-1.RLTS.T39380A2915472.en).

![レモンザメという名前の対応を学名で区別した図](https://silovar-uk.github.io/myessays/assets/lemon-shark-two-species-name-map-2026.svg)

Diagram: Japanese and English common names can refer to **different species**. Check the scientific name before using a study. Sources: [WoRMS](https://www.marinespecies.org/aphia.php?id=217356&p=taxdetails) · [Florida Museum](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/discover-fish/species-profiles/sicklefin-lemon-shark/).

Identification needs more than color. The body is broad, the snout is blunt, and the **two dorsal fins are almost the same size**. In many related sharks, the second dorsal fin is much smaller. 黄色っぽい体色だけでは、水中の光や環境の違いで誤認する。See [Okinawa Churaumi Aquarium](https://churaumi.okinawa/fishbook/00000496/).

**Identification checklist:** 1. Scientific name → 2. Geographic range → 3. Relative size of two dorsal fins → 4. Fin curvature and snout → 5. Color as supporting evidence. A single photo cannot always establish a species identification.

## 守｜The yellowish body belongs to a coastal way of life

A large individual may exceed three meters, with a maximum record near 3.8m. The species uses coral reefs, sandy lagoons and mangrove-fringed coasts across the Indo-Pacific, from the surface to roughly 92m. Its diet includes fish, rays and smaller sharks. **Slow swimming does not mean safe to approach.** [FishBase](https://www.fishbase.se/Fieldguide/FieldGuideSummary.php?c_code=036&genusname=Negaprion&speciesname=acutidens) · [Florida Museum](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/discover-fish/species-profiles/sicklefin-lemon-shark/).

To understand a shark, look beyond its teeth and follow its whole life cycle. This is a **viviparous** shark: young develop inside the mother and are born as pups. Reported gestation is around 10–11 months, with litters often described as roughly 1–13 pups. These are ranges, not a fixed production schedule. [FishBase](https://www.fishbase.se/country/CountrySpeciesSummary.php?c_code=356&id=896) · [Moorea breeding study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0073899).

## 守｜The 1981 Japanese record was about evidence, not just naming

The scientific description traces back to **Eduard Rüppell in 1837**. In Japan, two females measuring about 2.6–2.7m were collected off Iriomote Island in November 1979. Yoshino and colleagues published the Japanese record in **1981**, proposing the Japanese name レモンザメ; the paper also reports embryos. This was a specimen-based claim, not a nickname invented for an aquarium display. [Original paper](https://u-ryukyu.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/2005282/files/No32p037.pdf) · [J-GLOBAL](https://jglobal.jst.go.jp/detail?JGLOBAL_ID=200902095621425091).

In Okinawa the shark is also called **マーブカー**, a local name explained as roughly “ordinary shark.” To a reader elsewhere, the species sounds exotic; to people living beside that sea, the name can sound everyday. **Names preserve different human relationships with the same animal.** [Shimane AQUAS](https://aquas.or.jp/friends/mythical-seazone/) · [Churaumi Aquarium](https://churaumi.okinawa/fishbook/00000496/).

## 破｜A 2026 study puts young sharks back into Okinawa's coastal story

A study published in September 2026 used surveys conducted around **Kuroshima, Okinawa, from 2017 to 2020**. Researchers measured **199 sharks**, tagged and released **157**, and recorded lengths from **61.2 to 135.0cm**. Young individuals dominated; sightings rose in warmer periods. [Pacific Conservation Biology, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1071/PC26025).

The paper suggests many young sharks may move elsewhere within about two years. But **199 sampled animals is not the total population**, and a warm-season catch peak does not prove climate-driven growth. The next question is about movement: **where do those juveniles go after leaving Kuroshima?** [Methods and conclusions](https://doi.org/10.1071/PC26025).

## 破｜Staying local does not always mean genetic isolation

In **Dongsha Island**, scientists genotyped **188 juveniles** sampled in 2016–2017. The sharks showed **site fidelity**—repeated use of familiar places—but the study found no significant fine-scale genetic separation among three sites and five year-cohorts. **Local behavior and genetic separation are different questions.** [Scientific Reports, 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-28186-y).

A separate Dongsha study followed sharks with **acoustic telemetry** from 2016 to 2023: underwater receivers detected signals from tagged animals. This reveals when and where sharks passed near the receivers. But **no detection is not proof of absence** outside the receiver network. [Chen and Huang, 2024](https://taiwania.ntu.edu.tw/pdf/tai.2024.69.275.pdf).

Consider a thought experiment: protect a nursery perfectly, but leave every adult migration route exposed to danger. That does not protect the whole life cycle. Protect the offshore sea while destroying the nursery, and the next generation may suffer. This is a **conservation inference**, not a measured outcome at one particular site.

## 破｜Shark social learning is real—but belongs to a different species

A 2013 experiment showed that juvenile **Negaprion brevirostris** performed better after observing trained demonstrator sharks than after observing untrained controls. That supports **social learning**. But the species was **ニシレモンザメ**, not Japanese レモンザメ. The distinction matters precisely because the finding is so compelling. [Guttridge et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22933179/).

Could both species learn in similar ways? Possibly—but that is a **hypothesis to test**, not a demonstrated result. Relatedness helps us formulate questions. It does not give us their answers.

## 破｜Feeding sharks for tourists can protect or disturb them

In Moorea, French Polynesia, tourist operators sometimes fed sharks to facilitate viewing. A 2010 study used **949 dives over 44 months** and photo-identified **36 adults**. Researchers observed growing site residency and aggressive interactions, raising concerns about changes in behavior and possible inbreeding risk. **This does not prove feeding alone caused every change.** [Clua et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.3354/meps08746).

Shark tourism can also make a living shark economically valuable, creating incentives to protect it rather than catch it. The real tension is this: **the method used to bring an animal into view may change the animal being viewed.** A credible management system must consider feeding frequency, individual movement, reproduction and safety—not visitor satisfaction alone. [Tourism economics study](https://doi.org/10.1071/mf10163).

## 破｜Globally endangered does not mean every region has the same trend

The **IUCN global assessment** lists N. acutidens as **Endangered (EN)**. A roughly **50–79% population reduction over three generations, around 50 years**, was estimated from multiple lines of evidence. This is **not** a literal worldwide census. [IUCN](https://www.iucnredlist.org/species/41836/173435545) · [IUCN/TRAFFIC analysis](https://www.traffic.org/site/assets/files/19065/iucn-traffic-cop19-full-analyses-1.pdf).

An Australian government report prints two labels side by side: **Global: Endangered; Australia: Least Concern.** Australian fisheries are managed differently, and the report found no evidence of local population decline. This is not a contradiction. **Conservation status depends on geographic scale.** [Australian government species report, 2023](https://www.fish.gov.au/docs/SharkReport/2023_FRDC_Negaprion_acutidens_final.pdf).

Major pressures include fishing and bycatch, relatively slow life history, and coastal habitat degradation. The species is listed under **CITES Appendix II**, which regulates international trade; it does **not** mean that all capture everywhere is automatically prohibited. Fisheries, trade and habitat protection work through different rules. [Australian government report](https://www.fish.gov.au/docs/SharkReport/2023_FRDC_Negaprion_acutidens_final.pdf).

## 離｜A nursery is both a habitat and a place where knowledge becomes possible

Shallow coastal waters used by young sharks are often called **nursery habitats**. But seeing one pup does not automatically prove a place is an essential nursery. Scientists must examine repeated use, survival, growth and movement. Genetics, receiver networks and mark–recapture studies each reveal different parts of the same coastal system.

Places with frequent research or tourism generate more sightings and data. That does not establish that sharks are absent from less-studied coasts. **Every conservation map partly reflects where humans chose to look.** The blank spaces matter too.

## 離｜Conservation needs life-cycle evidence, not only sympathy

A useful **proposal** is to assess coastal development using five connected questions: nursery use, movement through life, overlap with fishing, breeding population turnover, and gaps in observation. Policies also differ by region. This is a research design—not proof that one particular intervention will work everywhere.

This idea reaches beyond sharks. Mangroves, river mouths, reefs and tidal shallows often support early life stages. Human planning tends to value what is visible now; ecology often depends on the route between life stages. **Protecting one animal means considering the places its whole life requires.**

## 結論｜A small naming puzzle changes the way we look at the sea

At first, the funny part was simply a shark named after a fruit. Then two species hid behind one familiar name. A 1979 specimen, a 2026 nursery survey, genetics in Dongsha, tourism in Moorea, and different regional conservation assessments changed the picture. The visible yellowish body was only the beginning. **The important things are often invisible in a photograph: movement, reproduction, evidence gaps and human pressure.**

Next time I see a lemon shark photograph, I want to ask: **Which species? Where did it grow? What does this coast make possible?** A naming problem has become a way to ask better ecological questions.

## 用語｜Ten terms for reading shark research

- **Cartilaginous fish（軟骨魚）**：Sharks and rays have mostly cartilage-based skeletons.
- **Scientific name（学名）**：A standard species identifier across languages.
- **Snout（吻）**：The front part of a fish's head.
- **Viviparity（胎生）**：Young develop inside the mother and are born as pups.
- **Nursery habitat（育成場）**：An environment used by juveniles during growth.
- **Site fidelity（場所への定着性）**：Repeated use of familiar locations.
- **Acoustic telemetry（音響テレメトリー）**：Tracking signals from tagged animals with underwater receivers.
- **Mark–recapture（標識再捕）**：Marking animals and studying later recaptures or sightings.
- **Bycatch（混獲）**：Animals captured unintentionally by fishing gear.
- **Endangered, EN（絶滅危惧）**：An IUCN threat category; geographic scope matters.

## Sources and limits｜Evidence, inference and uncertainty

**Primary evidence used:** a 1981 Japanese specimen paper, a 2026 Kuroshima survey, 2023 Dongsha genetics, 2024 telemetry, Moorea studies from 2010 and 2013, and official conservation assessments. Each claim links to its source. Research cutoff: **25 October 2026**. No original fieldwork or researcher interviews were conducted.

**Limits:** the social-learning experiment concerns the related species N. brevirostris. Global and regional threat assessments use different scopes. Decline percentages are estimates, not a worldwide count. Tourism studies raise concerns without proving every causal mechanism. Ranges of size and litter numbers were not turned into false precision.
