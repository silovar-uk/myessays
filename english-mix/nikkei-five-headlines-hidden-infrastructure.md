# Read today's newspaper through five things that are not the main characters
## GPU collateral, HDD thin films, a self-improving lab, soy-sauce factories, and deep-sea mud

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Looking across the September 26, 2026 newspaper, one thing felt odd. AI, semiconductors, resources, overseas markets—大きな主語はいくらでもある。Yet the more I researched, the more interesting the supporting cast became. A GPU（Graphics Processing Unit）is turning from「計算する部品」into collateral; generative AI is driving investment in material used to form magnetic layers on HDD platters; soy sauce is getting a third U.S. factory; and Japanese resource development is lifting mud from 5,569 meters below the sea. The side characters are extremely busy.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
So instead of ranking headlines by importance, I used one simple method:「主役を一段ずらす」—shift the protagonist down one layer. For AI, look not only at models but at financing and storage. For food, look not only at taste but at factories. For resources, look not only at reserves but at how material physically reaches a ship. Peel one layer off the headline and inspect the mechanism that makes the headline possible.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
The five stories are GPU-backed data-center financing, JX Advanced Metals' expansion of magnetic-material capacity for HDD media, Sakana AI's Recursive Self-Improvement lab, Kikkoman's third U.S. plant, and rare-earth mud around Minamitorishima. All five appeared as related themes in the September 26, 2026 Nihon Keizai Shimbun morning edition, and the factual core here was checked against primary materials.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
These stories belong to different industries, but「new demand」is too rough a description. When demand becomes real, an ecosystem has to change around it: how money is lent, how materials are made, how research is run, where production sits, and how extracted matter travels into processing. When a trend crosses from news into industry, these boring-looking layers often deform first.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
So these five stories are less about「what is trending?」and more about「what is becoming infrastructure?」. Shift the protagonist one layer and AI, soy sauce, and rare earths suddenly become stories about contracts, pipes, thin films, and factories. Strangely, that makes the shape of change easier to see.

## 1. A GPU is no longer only a computer component; it is becoming a financeable asset

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
The first strange point is simple. A GPU is supposed to compute, yet financial institutions are now underwriting enormous loans around fleets of GPUs and the infrastructure attached to them. Those black boxes in server halls are being read in the language of finance—as assets expected to generate future cash flows, somewhat like industrial equipment or aircraft.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
AI cloud company CoreWeave closed a financing facility of up to $8.5 billion in March 2026. It is a delayed draw term loan（DDTL）, meaning capital can be drawn as qualifying infrastructure is deployed. CoreWeave said the facility was the first financing backed by high-performance-computing infrastructure and an associated customer contract to receive investment-grade ratings. In August, it added another $2.6 billion facility with an approximately five-year maturity even though the underlying customer contracts average about three years.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
In a 2026 investor presentation filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission（SEC）, CoreWeave describes asset-level financing secured by long-term customer contracts plus the related GPUs, property and equipment, and data-center leases. The March facility allowed about $7.5 billion of initial borrowing capacity, expandable to $8.5 billion as the underlying assets stabilized. The August facility also contemplates either renewing contracts or re-leasing capacity to new customers after the original contracts end.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
This does **not** mean「GPU一枚を質屋へ持っていけば融資が出る」. Lenders are looking at a package: the GPUs, power and facilities needed to run them, customer contracts, and cash flows from usage. Still, once a loan can outlast the initial customer contract, the lender is also underwriting residual value and renewal risk—whether that compute can be sold again after the first deal ends.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
AI becoming an industry is not only a story about better models. It also means banks and asset managers can ask「How many years will this GPU fleet earn?」「Can it be re-leased?」「Which contracts make the debt repayable?」and build a financing model around the answers. Technology starts to look like infrastructure when it appears not only on engineers' performance charts but also on lenders' repayment schedules.

## 2. AI growth is increasing production of material used to coat HDD platters

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
When we hear「AI demand」, GPUs come to mind first. But on September 25, 2026, JX Advanced Metals announced more capacity for something much less glamorous: magnetic-material sputtering targets used to form recording layers on HDD media. 名前が長い。And as an AI story, it is remarkably close to the spinning disk.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
A sputtering target is the source material used to create a thin film. Ions strike the target in a vacuum, ejecting atoms that deposit on the disk surface and form the recording layer. JX Advanced Metals plans continuous capacity expansion over the next three years, bringing production capacity for these HDD materials to 1.5 times the 2025 level by mid-2029.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
The company points to the rapid spread of generative AI and cloud services, which is increasing the amount of data handled by data centers, and says HDDs remain important for efficiently storing huge volumes of data. Recording technology is also shifting from perpendicular magnetic recording（PMR）toward heat-assisted magnetic recording（HAMR）, which locally heats the recording layer with a laser to enable higher density. That transition requires new materials on the media side as well.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
This resolves the first question:「SSDの時代なのに、why HDD?」Storage is not a winner-takes-all contest. Fast-access storage and economical bulk storage play different roles. AI creates not only computation but also training data, generated outputs, logs, checkpoints, and backups—things that need to remain somewhere. The newer AI becomes, the more a veteran technology, magnetic disk storage, can need another round of density improvement.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
If we stare only at the large category「AI-related companies」, GPU vendors dominate the view. But implementation extends downward through layers: model → compute → network → storage → material. The JX Advanced Metals story suggests that to see the industrial footprint of a growth market, sometimes you need to walk down not one layer but three.

## 3. Sakana AI made the process of AI research itself an object of self-improvement

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
The third story sounds scarier than it needs to:「AIがAIを作る」. If we stop at that phrase, the claim gets inflated very quickly. Sakana AI's new research group is not evidence that an all-powerful AI is already rewriting itself without limit. Its target is recursive self-improvement（RSI）—using AI to automate and improve parts of the loop by which AI research proposes, tests, evaluates, and iterates.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Sakana AI describes a shift from static AI tools toward research systems that can propose hypotheses, design and run experiments, and feed results into the next round of improvement. The dedicated group is the RSI Lab. In September 2026, Jürgen Schmidhuber joined as Chief Scientific Advisor. According to Sakana AI, his 1987 thesis already described a program capable of improving its own learning algorithm.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
Sakana AI has previously published work on combining multiple large language models, systems that modify code while searching, and「AI Scientist」workflows that automate stages from research-idea generation through experiments and paper production. The new lab positions these not merely as isolated projects but as parts of one larger question: how can AI improve the AI-development process itself? The company also explicitly lists safeguards, observability, and publication of negative results among the research concerns.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
The interesting shift is that the object of optimization is expanding beyond model weights or code into「研究という仕事の手順」. If research changes from a straight line—humans build a model—to a loop where AI proposes experiments, evaluates them, and generates the next experiments, then the unit of competition may also shift from「best single model」toward「how cheaply, safely, and repeatedly can the improvement loop run?」. That is an interpretation of the research direction, not evidence that unlimited self-improvement has been achieved.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
A question that was highly abstract in 1987, when compute was far more limited, has become the operating premise of a Tokyo research group 39 years later. The new thing may be less「AI gets smarter」than「the work of making AI smarter is itself becoming machine-work」. In the same way factory automation changed the production process, AI research is taking a step toward the industrialization of its own research loop.

## 4. Soy sauce became global not only by being exported, but by being brewed locally

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
Kikkoman's third U.S. plant looks at first like an ordinary overseas-expansion story. But soy sauce becomes interesting if we treat it as a slightly awkward logistics product. It is liquid, heavy, used repeatedly, and made through fermentation. If global demand becomes ordinary demand, shipping every bottle from Japan forever is not an especially elegant system.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
Kikkoman began shipments from its Walworth, Wisconsin plant in 1973, added Folsom, California in 1998, and held the grand-opening ceremony for its third U.S. plant in Jefferson, Wisconsin in 2026. Construction began in April 2024, brewing started in May 2026, and shipments are scheduled to begin in October.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
The Jefferson site covers about 405,000 square meters, with planned investment of about $560 million over ten years. Kikkoman says North American soy-sauce demand continues to grow and shipments from the existing Walworth plant have been rising. The new plant is meant not only to expand supply but also to produce a wider variety of products and strengthen supply stability. North America is the company's largest overseas market.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
Here「海外進出」changes meaning. Exporting requires getting a product across a border. Local production embeds capacity into the destination itself: ingredients, water, labor, quality control, logistics, employment, and relationships with the surrounding community. The fact that U.S. production, begun 53 years ago, has reached a third plant shows globalization from the production side—not just soy sauce traveling as a Japanese import, but a product being manufactured continuously inside North American demand.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
When culture travels, restaurants and dish names get the attention. But depth of adoption can show up in factories. A novelty can survive on imports. Something used day after day creates reasons to make it nearby and reliably. If we measure the internationalization of soy sauce not only by「where is it known?」but also by「where is it brewed?」, we can see the point where culture becomes industrial infrastructure.

## 5. A Japanese "mine" can now mean lifting 50 tons of mud from 5,569 meters below the sea

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
The last story has the strangest mental image.「鉱山」usually suggests digging into a mountain. Around Minamitorishima, however, the resource is mixed into mud beneath the seabed. In the 2026 trial, the task was to send pipe more than five kilometers down and continuously lift that mud back to a ship.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
The Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology（JAMSTEC）, working under a Cabinet Office Strategic Innovation Promotion Program, used the deep-sea scientific drilling vessel「ちきゅう」for a mining-system connection test in Japan's exclusive economic zone around Minamitorishima from January to February 2026. The system breaks up seabed mud and raises it through pipes in a closed-circulation design intended to reduce the spread of suspended material into the surrounding water.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
On February 1, the system achieved continuous mud lifting from a point with a pipe length of 5,569 meters, and over three days recovered rare-earth mud from three layers. After excluding added seawater, the recovered mud weighed about 50 tons. Analysis found that about 54% of the total rare-earth content consisted of medium and heavy rare earths such as yttrium, gadolinium, and dysprosium. JAMSTEC plans a roughly one-month full-scale mining test from February 2027, followed by dewatering on Minamitorishima, transport to mainland Japan, and trials of separation, refining, and smelting.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
This is where the distance between「resource exists」and「resource can be used」becomes visible. Mud containing rare earths is not yet an industry. It has to be recovered reliably from more than five kilometers down, transported, dewatered, moved again, separated and refined, while waste and environmental effects are managed—and the economics still have to work. The 2026 result is a major technical step in continuous lifting, not proof that commercial production is already viable. The program plans an overall assessment including economics by March 2028.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
Being「資源小国」is not determined only by what lies underground. A resource becomes usable through a connected system of exploration, extraction, transport, separation, refining, and environmental management. Minamitorishima makes the definition of a mine shift from a place to a system. There may be no mountain, but if ship, pipe, processing, and logistics connect, a resource industry can begin to take shape.

## 6. Put the five together, and the boundary between trend and infrastructure becomes visible

<!-- level:4 role:claim -->
At first, I meant to pick five interesting headlines from today's paper. After researching them, I ended up with something else: five stories about「how new demand becomes ordinary enough to operate」rather than five stories about new technology itself.

<!-- level:2 role:description -->
GPUs acquire financing structures. HDDs need new recording-layer materials. AI research gets a system for iterating the research process itself. Soy sauce gets a third local factory. Rare earths need an end-to-end route from the deep seabed to refining. All of these sit one layer outside the glamorous product or service.

<!-- level:1 role:evidence -->
And none is a one-shot invention. CoreWeave has repeatedly expanded its financing structures. JX Advanced Metals followed a June capacity announcement with another expansion in September. Kikkoman has produced in the United States since 1973. Minamitorishima's 2026 test is one stage in development underway since 2018, followed by a 2027 mining test and a 2028 integrated assessment. Sakana AI frames recursive self-improvement as a modern foundation-model continuation of research ideas reaching back to the 1980s.

<!-- level:3 role:analysis -->
ここからはinterpretation. A trend can begin when attention arrives. Infrastructure exists when the system keeps running even without constant attention. That requires financeable assets, mass-producible materials, repeatable improvement loops, local production, and physical logistics. The more a novelty settles into society, the more its news moves from spectacular invention toward boring operations.

<!-- level:5 role:implication -->
So next time a headline says「AI」「global expansion」「resource development」, I want to move the subject down one layer. Who lends the money? Which material becomes scarce? Which workflow gets automated? Where does the factory go? What physically has to move, and how? The most interesting thing in today's newspaper was not the protagonists. It was the supporting infrastructure beginning to work hard enough to make those protagonists ordinary.
